# Scam Engineer #06: "Medicare arıyor": açık kayıt döneminin sahte aramaları

- **Kanal:** Scam Engineer (@thescamengineer)
- **Yayın:** 2 Aralık 2026 (Medicare açık kayıt döneminin son haftası, 15 Ekim – 7 Aralık; konular.md, 4. hafta)
- **Kitle:** ABD, 40+ yaş. Özellikle 65 yaşa yaklaşanlar ve yaşlı ebeveynleri olanlar.
- **Ton:** Sakin, saygılı. Gerçek Medicare kurumunu kötülemiyor, aksine resmî kanalları gösteriyor.
- **Hedef süre:** ~8 dk

## Neden bu konu (not)
- Açık kayıt dönemi 7 Aralık'ta bitiyor. İnsanlar gerçekten plan değiştirdiği için kafa karışıklığı yüksek ve dolandırıcılar bu karışıklığın içine saklanıyor.
- Michigan sigorta düzenleyicisi 29 Ekim 2026'da uyardı: arayanlar "Part D'ye katılmazsanız Medicare'iniz gider" diyor ve bu doğru değil. BBB 12 Ekim'de benzer bir uyarı yaptı.
- Nomorobo, Mayıs 2026'da Medicare'le ilgili yaklaşık 104 milyon arama tespit etti ve Ağustos'tan Eylül'e %25 artış ölçtü.
- Telekom açısı: ekranda "MEDICARE" yazmasının neden kanıt olmadığı (arayan adı bir veritabanından okunuyor). Spoofing videosuna doğal bir gönderme var ama konu tekrarı değil.

> 🎬 **Dramatizasyon:** "Dorothy" **temsilî bir karakter**. Arama metni Michigan DIFS, BBB ve Nomorobo'nun anlattığı gerçek senaryolardan derlendi. Ekranda "Reconstruction – composite character" yaz. Medicare logosunu veya gerçek kart görselini **kullanma**, kurgusal bir "health card" grafiği kullan.

## Senaryo

### [SAHNE 1 | Gelen arama ekranı: arayan adı "MEDICARE" (kurgusal arayüz), telefon titreşiyor | 0:00–0:13]
> The caller says you'll lose your Medicare unless you act today — and Medicare would never make that call. By the end, you'll know what Medicare never does by phone, and why your number is worth stealing.

### [SAHNE 2 | Gerçek görsel: telefon santrali / kablo dolabı (Wikimedia Commons: "Category:Telephone exchanges") | 0:13–0:19]
> I'm a telecom engineer, and I've spent my career inside the networks these calls travel through.

### [SAHNE 3 | RECONSTRUCTION: Mutfak masası; yanında posta yığını, plan karşılaştırma broşürleri; yaşlı bir kadının eli telefonu tutuyor, yüz yok (Pexels stok) | 0:19–0:52]
> Meet Dorothy. She's seventy-one, and it's open enrollment season. Her kitchen table is covered in plan brochures, and she's honestly not sure if she needs to change anything.
>
> Her phone rings. The screen says "MEDICARE."
>
> A polite caller explains that her member profile hasn't been transferred into Medicare's national database for next year. Unless she enrolls in a new drug plan today, she could lose her coverage. To fix it, they just need to "verify" her Medicare number.
>
> It sounds official. It's the right time of year. And the screen literally says Medicare. The caller even knows her first name and her town — details that are easy to buy, or find online, but that make a stranger sound like they've already got her file open.
>
> But here's our first question. Dorothy's Medicare number isn't a bank card. You can't buy groceries with it. So why would a scammer want it so badly?

### [SAHNE 4 | Animasyon: Arama → operatör → "Caller name database" → telefon ekranında isim beliriyor; yanında soru işareti (kendi grafik) | 0:52–2:05]
> Before we answer that, let's deal with the word on the screen.
>
> In my world, the name you see when a call comes in usually isn't sent with any proof. Your phone or your carrier looks the number up in a directory, or in your own contacts, and shows whatever name it finds. And as we covered in my video on caller ID spoofing, the number itself can be faked. The Better Business Bureau puts it simply: caller ID is not proof that a call is legitimate.
>
> And these calls are not rare. The call-blocking company Nomorobo estimated it tracked around 104 million Medicare-related calls in May 2026 alone. More than 7.6 million Americans received one particular pitch: the "national database" story Dorothy just heard. Nomorobo then measured a twenty-five percent increase in Medicare-related spam and scam calls between August and September.
>
> Which raises the next question: why do these calls spike right now?

### [SAHNE 5 | Takvim animasyonu: 15 Ekim → 7 Aralık arası vurgulanıyor; kurgusal "health card" grafiği, üzerinde "NEW CARD?" | 2:05–3:15]
> Because open enrollment is real. Between October 15th and December 7th, people with Medicare can review and change their health and drug plans. Plans send mail. Ads fill the TV. Everyone is talking about coverage.
>
> That noise is perfect camouflage.
>
> On October 29th, Michigan's insurance regulator warned about callers claiming people must join a Part D drug plan or lose their Medicare. The state said neither claim is true. The BBB reported callers and texts posing as Medicare staff — in one case asking a consumer to pay to "verify" information.
>
> Another favourite is the new-card story: you need a new card, a plastic card, a chip card, and there's a small fee. The Senior Medicare Patrol lists those as warning signs. Medicare doesn't charge for your card.
>
> So now, back to the question we parked. What is Dorothy's number actually worth?

### [SAHNE 6 | Animasyon: Medicare numarası → sahte faturalar (genetik test, tıbbi ekipman ikonları) → "Medicare pays" (kendi grafik); HHS-OIG uyarısı metin grafiği | 3:15–4:30]
> A Medicare number is a billing key. With it, a dishonest provider can bill Medicare for tests, equipment or services you never received.
>
> This isn't hypothetical. The Department of Health and Human Services' Office of Inspector General has warned for years about scams where people are offered "free" genetic tests or medical equipment by phone, in exchange for their Medicare number — and Medicare is then billed.
>
> That's why the scammer was so patient with Dorothy. They don't need her bank account, and they don't need her to send a single dollar. Her number can be billed, again and again, often in ways she'd never notice unless she read her statements.
>
> It can also be used alongside other personal details for identity theft. Every extra fact you share on a call — your date of birth, your Social Security number, your bank — makes the package more valuable.
>
> But here's a fair question. Plans and agents do sell coverage by phone. So can a real one ever call you?

### [SAHNE 7 | Grafik: "Medicare will / won't" iki sütun; sakin renkler | 4:30–5:40]
> Here are the rules, as plainly as I can put them.
>
> Medicare itself won't call you to sell you anything. It won't call to ask for, or "verify," your Medicare number. And it won't enroll you in a plan over the phone unless you called first.
>
> Private plans and their agents have marketing rules too. Under Medicare's marketing rules, they generally aren't allowed to cold-call you. They may contact you if you've given them permission, or if you're already one of their members.
>
> And nobody can take your Medicare away because you didn't answer a phone call or join a drug plan by Friday.
>
> If you genuinely want help comparing plans, there are free, unbiased options: 1-800-MEDICARE, and your State Health Insurance Assistance Program, known as SHIP, which offers free one-on-one counselling.
>
> So when the phone rings and the screen says Medicare — what do you actually say?

### [SAHNE 8 | Büyük, sade yazı: "I don't share my Medicare number on calls I didn't make." | 5:40–6:50]
> Here's the sentence: "I don't share my Medicare number on calls I didn't make. I'll call Medicare myself." Then hang up.
>
> You're not being rude. You're following the same rule Medicare follows. And if the caller pushes back — says it's urgent, says you'll lose coverage, says don't hang up — that pressure is the clearest sign you could ask for. Real agencies don't mind you calling them back.
>
> Then, if you want to check, call 1-800-MEDICARE — that's 1-800-633-4227 — or log in to your account at Medicare.gov. If the call was real, they'll know about it.
>
> And here's a habit that turns you into your own fraud detector: read your Medicare Summary Notices, or check your claims online. Look for services, tests or equipment you never received. That's where stolen numbers show up.
>
> If something looks wrong, call the Senior Medicare Patrol at 1-877-808-2468. They'll connect you with your state's program for one-on-one help.

### [SAHNE 9 | RECONSTRUCTION: Dorothy telefonu kapatıyor, broşürlerin arasından bir SHIP el ilanı çıkarıyor (kurgusal); yüz yok | 6:50–7:55]
> And if you've already given your number, like Dorothy almost did? Don't panic. Call 1-800-MEDICARE, tell them what happened, and ask what they recommend. Watch your statements closely over the next few months. And contact the Senior Medicare Patrol for help.
>
> So, our questions. Why does a scammer want a Medicare number? Because it can be billed. Why does the screen say "Medicare"? Because caller names aren't proof, and numbers can be faked. Why now? Because open enrollment is real, and real confusion is the best disguise. Can a real plan call you? Only within strict rules, and Medicare itself won't call to ask for your number.
>
> As an engineer, here's the part I like: these scams depend on you staying on the line. The moment you say "I'll call Medicare myself," the whole script falls apart.
>
> That's how this scam works — so it never works on you.

## Shorts (3 adet, **üç farklı format**)

### Short 1: QUIZ (A/B/C) (35 sn)
- **Ekran:** Arama ekranı "MEDICARE". Soru: "What should you share?"
- **VO:** Your phone says "Medicare." The caller wants to verify your Medicare number so you don't lose coverage. What do you share? A) Just the number, since they're Medicare. B) Only the last four digits. C) Nothing. Hang up and call 1-800-MEDICARE yourself. … It's C. Medicare doesn't call to verify your number, and the name on your screen isn't proof. Your Medicare number can be used to bill for services you never got.

### Short 2: KISA HİKÂYE (40 sn)
- **Görsel:** Mutfak masası, broşürler, çalan telefon.
- **VO:** Dorothy was comparing plans when "Medicare" called. Her profile wasn't in the national database, they said. Join a new drug plan today, or lose coverage. Just verify your Medicare number. She almost did. Then she remembered: Medicare doesn't call to ask for your number. She hung up, called 1-800-MEDICARE, and learned her coverage was fine. The only thing at risk was her number. *(Ekranda: "Composite character")*

### Short 3: GERÇEK Mİ SAHTE Mİ? (30 sn)
- **Ekran:** Üç ifade, her biri için "REAL or FAKE?"
- **VO:** Real or fake? One: "You'll lose Medicare if you don't join a Part D plan by Friday." Fake. Michigan regulators say that claim is false. Two: "Open enrollment runs October 15th to December 7th." Real. Three: "Your new chip-enabled Medicare card costs nine ninety-nine." Fake. Medicare doesn't charge for cards. When in doubt, hang up and call 1-800-MEDICARE yourself.
