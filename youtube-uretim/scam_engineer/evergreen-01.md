### [SAHNE 3 | RECONSTRUCTION: Mutfak, sabah kahvesi, telefonu tutan eller, yüz yok (Pexels). Köşede "Composite character" | Kesme: 3–4 sn | 0:16–0:45]
> Maria's phone buzzes three times before breakfast. A package is "on hold" until she updates her address. Her bank flags a "suspicious charge." And a stranger writes: "Hi, is this Anna?"
>
> One might be real. Maybe none are.
>
> So how do you tell, in seconds, which texts to trust?
>
> Most people try to judge how a message looks. That's exactly the test scammers are best at passing.

### [SAHNE 4 | Animasyon: Sahte ve gerçek mesaj yan yana; logo, yazım ve ton neredeyse aynı (kurgusal) | 0:45–1:55]
> Here's the uncomfortable truth. A scam text can look perfect. Copying a company's name, tone and even its exact wording costs nothing. Spelling mistakes used to be a giveaway. Now, many scam texts are cleanly written.
>
> According to the Federal Trade Commission, the most common scam texts reported in 2024 were fake package delivery problems, fake job offers, fake bank fraud alerts, fake unpaid tolls, and "wrong number" messages that turn into long conversations. Reported losses were more than five times higher than in 2020 — and the FTC says most fraud never gets reported at all.
>
> There's another reason appearance fails. Scam texts are sent to huge numbers of phones at once. The sender doesn't need to fool you specifically. They only need a tiny share of people to be busy, tired or worried at the exact moment the message arrives — waiting for a real delivery, say, or just back from shopping.
>
> So judging by appearance is a losing game. You need a test that doesn't depend on how convincing the message is.
>
> Before the test, though, it helps to know what these messages have in common. What are they all trying to make you do?

### [SAHNE 5 | Dört kart sırayla açılıyor: "SURPRISE", "HURRY", "TAP/REPLY", "PAY/SHARE" (kendi grafik) | 1:55–3:10]
> Almost every scam text tries to move you through four steps.
>
> One: surprise. It's about something you didn't expect — a package you can't place, a charge you don't recognize, a job you never applied for.
>
> Two: hurry. A deadline, a late fee, a locked account. Urgency stops you from checking.
>
> Three: a path. A link to tap, a number to call, or a request to reply — sometimes just "reply Y." That path is chosen by the sender, not by you.
>
> Four: the ask. Your card, your password, a one-time code, a "small fee," or simply a conversation that will later turn into a request for money.
>
> Real companies send real texts too. Delivery updates are real. Bank alerts are real. So the four signs don't prove a text is fake. They tell you when to switch to the test.
>
> And there's a telecom detail that helps here. Where did the text actually come from?

### [SAHNE 6 | Animasyon: Üç gönderen türü: "short code (5–6 digits)", "10-digit number", "email address" (kendi grafik) | 3:10–4:20]
> When a business sends texts in the U.S., they often come from a short code — a five- or six-digit number — or a registered business line. A delivery or bank alert from a random personal number, or from an email address showing up in your messages app, is a warning sign.
>
> But it's only a warning sign. Sender details can be faked, and some scams arrive through internet messaging apps that never pass through your carrier's text filters at all.
>
> The U.S. Postal Inspection Service, for example, says USPS doesn't text customers with links unless they've signed up for tracking with a tracking number. That's the kind of rule that helps: it comes from the company itself, not from the message.
>
> The same goes for banks. Many banks state publicly, on their own websites, that they will never ask you to share a one-time code by text or phone, and never ask you to move money to "protect" it. Look up those rules on your own bank's website, not in a message.
>
> Which brings us to the test. It's one sentence long. What is it?

### [SAHNE 7 | Büyük, sade yazı: "Don't use their path. Use yours." ve yanında üç simge: uygulama, yer imi, kartın arkası | 4:20–5:30]
> Don't use their path. Use yours.
>
> If a text says your package is held, don't tap the link. Open the carrier's app you already have, or type the tracking site yourself.
>
> If a text says your bank saw fraud, don't reply and don't call the number in the message. Open your banking app, or call the number printed on your card.
>
> If a stranger says "wrong number," don't continue the chat. The FTC says these conversations can slowly turn into investment and romance scams.
>
> Notice what the test doesn't require. You don't need to spot a typo, decode a web address or know the latest scam. You don't need to be technical at all. You only need to refuse one thing: the route the message hands you.
>
> That's the whole test, and it takes about thirty seconds. If the problem is real, it'll be waiting for you in the real account. If it isn't there, you've just avoided the scam without needing to know which one it was.
>
> So what do you do with the fake ones?

### [SAHNE 8 | Telefon ekranında "Report Junk" seçeneği ve 7726'ya iletme animasyonu (kurgusal arayüz) | 5:30–6:40]
> Three quick steps, in this order.
>
> First, report it. On most phones, you can forward the text to 7726 — that spells SPAM — which helps your carrier block similar messages. Or use the "Report junk" option in your messages app.
>
> Second, if you want to help investigators, report it at ReportFraud.ftc.gov.
>
> Third, delete it. Don't reply, not even "STOP" to a sender you don't recognize, and not even "Y." A reply tells the sender your number is live.
>
> If you already tapped a link or typed something in before reading this far, don't worry about being embarrassed. Act calmly: change that password, call your bank on the number on your card, and watch for unfamiliar charges. We have a separate step-by-step video for exactly that situation.
>
> And one setting worth turning on today: on an iPhone, "Filter Unknown Senders" moves messages from people you don't know into a separate list. Android phones have built-in spam protection in the messages app too. Neither is perfect, but both reduce how often you have to make the decision at all.

### [SAHNE 9 | Maria'nın sabahı: üç mesaj tek tek siliniyor; sonra kargo uygulaması açılıyor, "No issues" (kurgusal) | 6:40–7:45]
> Back to Maria's three texts.
>
> The package? She opened her shipping app. No hold, no fee. Deleted.
>
> The fraud alert? She opened her bank's app. No suspicious charge. Reported and deleted.
>
> "Is this Anna?" No reply. Deleted.
>
> She didn't need to decide which message was fake. She just refused to use the path any of them offered — and that's what makes the test work on every text, even ones that haven't been invented yet.
>
> Surprise, hurry, a path, an ask: when you see them, switch to the test. Don't use their path. Use yours.
>
> That's how this scam works — so it never works on you.
