## Senaryo

### [SAHNE 1 | 0–3 sn: Telefon titreşerek çalıyor, ekranda arayan adı "MEDICARE" (kurgusal arayüz) → 3–7 sn: takvimde 7 Aralık daire içine alınıyor → 7–12 sn: kurgusal bir sağlık kartı ve kilit simgesi | Kesme: 2–3 sn | 0:00–0:12]
> The caller says you'll lose your Medicare unless you act today — and Medicare would never make that call. Here's why your number is worth stealing.

### [SAHNE 2 | Telefon santrali / kablo dolabı (Wikimedia Commons: "Category:Telephone exchanges") | 0:12–0:16]
> This channel is made by a telecom engineer.

### [SAHNE 3 | RECONSTRUCTION: Mutfak masası, plan broşürleri, telefonu tutan yaşlı bir el, yüz yok (Pexels). Köşede "Composite character" | Kesme: 3–4 sn | 0:16–0:45]
> Dorothy, seventy-one, is comparing plans. Her phone says "MEDICARE." A polite caller says her profile wasn't transferred into Medicare's national database. Join a new drug plan today, or risk losing coverage. They just need to "verify" her Medicare number.
>
> Her Medicare number can't buy groceries. So why would a scammer want it so badly?
>
> The caller even knows her name and her town.

### [SAHNE 4 | Animasyon: Arama → operatör → "Caller name database" → telefon ekranında isim beliriyor, yanında soru işareti (kendi grafik) | 0:45–2:00]
> Before answering that, let's deal with the word on the screen.
>
> Inside phone networks, the name you see when a call comes in usually isn't sent with any proof. Your phone or your carrier looks the number up in a directory, or in your contacts, and shows whatever name it finds. And as we covered in our video on caller ID spoofing, the number itself can be faked. The Better Business Bureau puts it simply: caller ID is not proof that a call is legitimate.
>
> Knowing her name and town doesn't prove anything either. Details like that are easy to buy, or find online, but they make a stranger sound like they've already got your file open.
>
> And these calls are not rare. The call-blocking company Nomorobo estimated it tracked around 104 million Medicare-related calls in May 2026 alone. More than 7.6 million Americans received one particular pitch: the "national database" story Dorothy just heard. Nomorobo then measured a twenty-five percent increase in Medicare-related spam and scam calls between August and September.
>
> Which raises the next question: why do these calls spike right now?

### [SAHNE 5 | Takvim animasyonu: 15 Ekim → 7 Aralık vurgulanıyor, bugünün tarihi (18 Kasım) işaretli; kurgusal "health card" grafiği, üstünde "NEW CARD?" | 2:00–3:10]
> Because open enrollment is real. Between October 15th and December 7th, people with Medicare can review and change their health and drug plans. As this video goes out, there are about three weeks left. Plans send mail. Ads fill the TV. Everyone is talking about coverage.
>
> That noise is perfect camouflage. When real plan changes are in the news, a call about "your plan for next year" doesn't sound strange. It sounds helpful. And a deadline that really exists makes a fake deadline — "today," "by Friday" — easier to believe.
>
> On October 29th, Michigan's insurance regulator warned about callers claiming people must join a Part D drug plan or lose their Medicare. The state said neither claim is true. The BBB reported callers and texts posing as Medicare staff, in one case asking a consumer to pay to "verify" information.
>
> Another favorite is the new-card story: you need a new card, a plastic card, a chip card, and there's a small fee. The Senior Medicare Patrol lists those as warning signs. Medicare doesn't charge for your card.
>
> So, back to the question we parked. What is Dorothy's number actually worth?

### [SAHNE 6 | Animasyon: Medicare numarası → sahte faturalar (genetik test, tıbbi ekipman ikonları) → "Medicare pays"; HHS-OIG uyarısı metin grafiği | 3:10–4:25]
> A Medicare number is a billing key. With it, a dishonest provider can bill Medicare for tests, equipment or services you never received.
>
> This isn't hypothetical. The Department of Health and Human Services' Office of Inspector General has warned for years about scams where people are offered "free" genetic tests or medical equipment by phone, in exchange for their Medicare number — and Medicare is then billed.
>
> That's why the scammer was so patient with Dorothy. They don't need her bank account, and they don't need her to send a single dollar. Her number can be billed again and again, often in ways she'd never notice unless she read her statements.
>
> It can also be combined with other personal details for identity theft. Every extra fact shared on a call — date of birth, Social Security number, bank — makes the package more valuable.
>
> But here's a fair question. Plans and agents do sell coverage by phone. So can a real one ever call you?

### [SAHNE 7 | Grafik: "Medicare will / won't" iki sütun, sakin renkler | 4:25–5:35]
> Here are the rules, as plainly as possible, so you can recognize a fake call in seconds.
>
> Medicare itself won't call to sell you anything. It won't call to ask for, or "verify," your Medicare number. And it won't enroll you in a plan over the phone unless you called first.
>
> Private plans and their agents have marketing rules too. Under Medicare's marketing rules, they generally aren't allowed to cold-call you. They may contact you if you've given them permission, or if you're already one of their members.
>
> And nobody can take your Medicare away because you didn't answer a phone call or join a drug plan by Friday. If you're happy with your current coverage, in most cases you don't have to do anything at all during open enrollment. Reviewing is wise. Rushing is not.
>
> If you genuinely want help comparing plans before December 7th, there are free, unbiased options: 1-800-MEDICARE, and your State Health Insurance Assistance Program, known as SHIP, which offers free one-on-one counseling.
>
> So when the phone rings and the screen says Medicare, what do you actually say?

### [SAHNE 8 | Büyük, sade yazı: "I don't share my Medicare number on calls I didn't make." | 5:35–6:45]
> Here's the sentence: "I don't share my Medicare number on calls I didn't make. I'll call Medicare myself." Then hang up.
>
> That isn't rude. It's the same rule Medicare follows. And if the caller pushes back — says it's urgent, says you'll lose coverage, says don't hang up — that pressure is the clearest sign you could ask for. Real agencies don't mind you calling them back.
>
> To check, call 1-800-MEDICARE — that's 1-800-633-4227 — or log in to your account at Medicare.gov. If the call was real, they'll know about it.
>
> And here's a habit that turns you into your own fraud detector: read your Medicare Summary Notices, or check your claims online. Look for services, tests or equipment you never received. That's where stolen numbers show up.
>
> If something looks wrong, call the Senior Medicare Patrol at 1-877-808-2468. They'll connect you with your state's program for one-on-one help.

### [SAHNE 9 | RECONSTRUCTION: Dorothy telefonu kapatıyor, broşürlerin arasından kurgusal bir SHIP el ilanı çıkarıyor; yüz yok | 6:45–7:50]
> And if you've already given your number, like Dorothy almost did? Don't panic. Call 1-800-MEDICARE, tell them what happened, and ask what they recommend. Watch your statements closely over the next few months. And contact the Senior Medicare Patrol for help.
>
> Dorothy hung up and called Medicare herself. Her coverage was fine. Nothing had been transferred anywhere, because there was no "national database" problem to fix. The only thing that had been at risk was her number.
>
> With three weeks of open enrollment left, the calls will keep coming. The name on the screen will say "Medicare." The caller will sound kind, and in a hurry. And every one of those scripts depends on you staying on the line.
>
> "I'll call Medicare myself." That's how this scam works — so it never works on you.
