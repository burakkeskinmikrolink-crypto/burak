> If a scammer has your Social Security number, four free steps can make it far less useful to them. Here's the order to do them in.

### [SAHNE 2 | Telefon santrali / kablo dolabı (Wikimedia Commons) | 0:12–0:16]
> This channel is made by a telecom engineer.

### [SAHNE 3 | RECONSTRUCTION: Oturma odası, sabit telefon ve not defteri, yüz yok (Pexels). Köşede "Composite character" | Kesme: 3–4 sn | 0:16–0:45]
> Gary, sixty-three, gets a call. The caller says his Social Security number has been "suspended" over suspicious activity, and asks him to confirm it.
>
> He reads it out. Then he hangs up, and his stomach drops.
>
> Is a stolen Social Security number gone forever — or can you still lock it down?
>
> The answer is better than most people expect. But the order matters.

### [SAHNE 4 | Animasyon: SSN kartından dört ok çıkıyor: "new credit", "tax refund", "benefits", "jobs" (kendi grafik) | 0:45–1:55]
> First, the "suspended number" story. The Social Security Administration's own fraud warnings say it will never call to tell you your number has been suspended, and that real officials won't threaten you or demand immediate payment. That call was the scam, not a warning about one.
>
> It's also worth knowing why the caller asked you to "confirm" the number. Often they already have part of it, from old data breaches, and need you to fill the gaps. Saying it out loud completes their file. That isn't foolish; the script is designed to sound like routine verification.
>
> So what can someone actually do with your number? The big risks are four: opening new credit in your name, filing a tax return in your name to collect a refund, using it for benefits, or using it to get a job.
>
> Here's the thing most people don't realize. Getting a brand-new Social Security number is rare and difficult. So the strategy isn't to replace the number. It's to make every door the number opens harder to walk through.
>
> And there's one place to start, which builds the plan for you. Where is it?

### [SAHNE 5 | Ekran kaydı tarzı: IdentityTheft.gov ana sayfası yerine sade metin grafiği "IdentityTheft.gov → Your recovery plan" (resmî logo yok) | 1:55–3:00]
> Step one: IdentityTheft.gov. It's free, and it's run by the Federal Trade Commission.
>
> You answer questions about what happened, and it creates a personal recovery plan: which companies to contact, in what order, with pre-filled letters you can use. If someone has already misused your number, you can file an official Identity Theft Report there, which helps when you dispute fraudulent accounts later.
>
> Even if nothing has happened yet, it's worth going through, because it turns a scary situation into a checklist you can finish at your own pace. Keep a copy of everything it gives you.
>
> If money has already been taken, or someone is clearly using your identity, you can also file a report with your local police department. Some banks and creditors ask for one, and the FTC's plan tells you when it's useful.
>
> But a plan doesn't stop someone opening a credit card tomorrow. What does?

### [SAHNE 6 | Üç kurgusal büro kutusu "Bureau 1 / 2 / 3", her birinde kilit kapanıyor (logo yok) | 3:00–4:15]
> Step two: freeze your credit.
>
> In the U.S., you can freeze your credit for free at each of the three national credit bureaus: Equifax, Experian and TransUnion. A freeze stops new lenders from seeing your credit report, which means most new accounts can't be opened in your name. It doesn't hurt your credit score, and you can lift it temporarily when you apply for something yourself.
>
> You have to do it at all three. Each takes a few minutes online or by phone.
>
> If you're not ready to freeze, there's a lighter option: a fraud alert. You contact one bureau, and it notifies the other two. Lenders are then expected to take extra steps to verify it's really you before opening an account.
>
> When you freeze, each bureau gives you a way to lift it later: usually an online account, sometimes a PIN. Write down how to reach each one and keep it somewhere safe, because you'll need it the next time you apply for a loan, a credit card or even some phone contracts.
>
> You don't need to pay anyone for this. The freeze is free by law.
>
> But a freeze doesn't stop the second big risk. What about your taxes?

### [SAHNE 7 | Takvim, "TAX SEASON" ve bir kilitli zarf (kendi grafik); IRS logosu yok | 4:15–5:20]
> Step three: get an Identity Protection PIN from the IRS.
>
> Since 2021, the IRS has let any taxpayer who can verify their identity opt in to an IP PIN — a six-digit number that must be on your federal tax return. Without it, a return filed with your Social Security number gets rejected. That blocks someone else from filing first and collecting your refund.
>
> You request it through the "Get an IP PIN" tool on IRS.gov. A new PIN is issued every year, so you'll need the current one each time you file. If you use a tax preparer, give them the PIN when you file. It's a small extra step that closes one of the most common doors identity thieves use.
>
> One door left — and it's the one people forget. Who controls your account at Social Security itself?

### [SAHNE 8 | Bir laptop ekranında sade metin "my Social Security — create account" (logo yok); AnnualCreditReport metin grafiği | 5:20–6:35]
> Step four: create your "my Social Security" account at ssa.gov — before someone else does.
>
> If a scammer opens that account first, they can see your records and may try to redirect benefits. When you create it yourself, you control it, and you can review your earnings record for jobs you never had.
>
> Then keep watching. You can get free credit reports from all three bureaus at AnnualCreditReport.com. Look for accounts, addresses or inquiries you don't recognize. Thieves sometimes wait months before using a number, so check regularly, not just once.
>
> If you're on Medicare, read your Medicare Summary Notices for services you never received. Your Social Security number and your Medicare number are separate, but scammers who have one often try for the other — we cover Medicare calls in a separate video.
>
> And if you shared more than your Social Security number — a bank account, a card, a password — call those companies too, using the numbers on your cards or statements, not any number the caller gave you.

### [SAHNE 9 | Gary'nin not defteri: dört madde tek tek işaretleniyor ✓ | 6:35–7:45]
> So, back to Gary. Here's exactly what he did that afternoon, in order.
>
> One: IdentityTheft.gov, for a plan and a record.
>
> Two: credit freezes at all three bureaus.
>
> Three: an IRS Identity Protection PIN.
>
> Four: his own "my Social Security" account, plus a reminder in his calendar to check his credit reports.
>
> What he didn't do matters too. He didn't pay anyone who offered to "fix" his Social Security number. Those offers are often a second scam aimed at the same people.
>
> And if this happened to a parent or grandparent, offer to sit with them while they do the four steps. Most of it takes an afternoon, and doing it together removes the embarrassment that stops many people from acting at all.
>
> A stolen number isn't the end of the story. It's the start of a checklist. And every step on it is free.
>
> That's how this scam works — so it never works on you.
