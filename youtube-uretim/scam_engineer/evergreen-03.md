### [SAHNE 3 | RECONSTRUCTION: Akşam, kanepe, telefonu tutan eller, yüz yok (Pexels). Köşede "Composite character" | Kesme: 3–4 sn | 0:16–0:45]
> Ruth, fifty-eight, taps a text about a "missed delivery." A page asks for a dollar-fifty redelivery fee. She types her card number. Then she sees the address bar, and freezes.
>
> So — what actually happened to her phone, and what should she do in the next ten minutes?
>
> Let's take it one door at a time, because the answer depends on which doors she opened.

### [SAHNE 4 | Animasyon: Telefon → internet → web sunucusu; "sayfa sadece gösterilir" (kendi grafik) | 0:45–1:55]
> First, what a link really does. When you tap a link in a text, your phone's browser asks a web server for a page and shows it to you. That's all. On a phone that's kept up to date, simply opening a page usually doesn't install anything by itself.
>
> The danger comes from what the page persuades you to do next: type something, approve something, or install something.
>
> Security researchers do occasionally find flaws that let a page do more than it should, which is exactly why software updates matter. But for everyday scam texts, the page's real goal is your information, not your phone.
>
> Notice the tiny fee, too. A dollar-fifty doesn't feel like a risk, which is exactly why it's asked for. The real target is the card details typed in to pay it — the fee itself is just the excuse.
>
> So Ruth's situation isn't "my phone is hacked." It's "a stranger may have my card number." That's a much more fixable problem.
>
> Now, which door did you open? There are really three.

### [SAHNE 5 | Kapı 1 vurgulanıyor: "OPENED ONLY" (kendi grafik) | 1:55–2:55]
> Door one: you only opened the page. You didn't type anything, approve anything or download anything.
>
> Close the tab. Don't go back to it out of curiosity. Then update your phone's operating system and your browser if updates are waiting — updates close the rare holes that pages can use.
>
> Before you delete the text, take a quick screenshot of it, including the sender's number and the link. If you later need to dispute a charge or file a report, that screenshot is your evidence.
>
> Report the text: forward it to 7726, which spells SPAM, or use "Report junk" in your messages app. Then delete it.
>
> That's it. For most people who tapped a link and stopped, that's the whole job. You don't need to reset your phone, change your number or buy anything. Just close, update, report and move on with your evening.
>
> But Ruth typed something. What changes then?

### [SAHNE 6 | Kapı 2 vurgulanıyor: "TYPED SOMETHING", alt maddeler: card · password · code (kendi grafik) | 2:55–4:20]
> Door two: you typed something into the page. What you typed decides the next call.
>
> If it was a **card number**, call the bank or card company using the number printed on your card — not any number from the text. Tell them you entered the card on a fraudulent site. They can block the card, issue a new one and watch for charges. Credit cards come with strong legal dispute rights in the U.S.; with debit cards, call even faster, because the money comes straight from your account.
>
> If it was a **password**, change it right away on the real website or app — and on any other account where you used the same password. Then turn on two-step verification, ideally with an authenticator app.
>
> If it was a **one-time code** from your bank, call the bank immediately. Codes are often used to approve something on another device — like adding your card to a digital wallet on someone else's phone. Ask the bank to check for new devices and wallets linked to your card.
>
> And if you typed personal details like your Social Security number, the FTC's IdentityTheft.gov builds a free recovery plan. We have a separate step-by-step video for that.

### [SAHNE 7 | Kapı 3 vurgulanıyor: "INSTALLED / ALLOWED SOMETHING" (kendi grafik); ayarlar ekranı yerine sade metin listesi | 4:20–5:35]
> Door three is less common, but important: the page got you to install or allow something.
>
> That could be an app downloaded from outside your phone's official store, a "profile" you were asked to install, or permission to send you notifications.
>
> On Android, check your list of installed apps for anything you don't recognize and uninstall it, and check which apps you've allowed to install other apps. On iPhone, look in Settings under General for VPN and Device Management, and remove any profile you didn't knowingly add. In your browser's settings, remove notification permission for any site you don't trust.
>
> Then run the security scan built into your phone, or a reputable security app, and change important passwords from a different device you trust.
>
> If you're unsure, a local phone store or a trusted technician can check it with you. There's no shame in asking.
>
> Which leaves the question people ask most often: how do you know whether anything was actually taken?

### [SAHNE 8 | Banka uygulaması bildirimleri (kurgusal), kredi raporu metin grafiği | 5:35–6:40]
> You watch, for a while. Check your bank and card statements for small, unfamiliar charges — scammers sometimes test a card with a tiny purchase first. Turn on transaction alerts in your banking app, so every charge sends you a notification.
>
> If you shared personal information beyond a card, check your credit reports for free at AnnualCreditReport.com, and consider a free credit freeze.
>
> Keep a simple note of what happened and when: the time you tapped the link, what you typed, who you called and what they said. If anything goes wrong later, that short timeline makes every conversation with your bank much faster.
>
> And report what happened at ReportFraud.ftc.gov. Reports help investigators spot patterns, even when they can't recover money for one person.
>
> One last warning: you may get a follow-up call or text "from your bank" offering to help — sometimes within minutes. Don't use that path. Hang up and call the number on your card. Scammers know which numbers just took the bait.

### [SAHNE 9 | Ruth'un telefonu: kart iptali onayı, yeni kart yolda (kurgusal); sakin mavi ekran | 6:40–7:50]
> Back to Ruth. She opened door two: she typed her card number.
>
> She closed the page, called the number on the back of her card, and the bank cancelled it and sent a new one. She turned on transaction alerts, forwarded the text to 7726 and reported it to the FTC. She didn't install anything, so door three stayed shut. Total time: about fifteen minutes.
>
> Two weeks later, a small charge she didn't recognize appeared on the old card number — and it was declined, because the card no longer existed.
>
> That's the real lesson. Clicking isn't the end of the story. Your next ten minutes are. Close the page, figure out which door you opened, and use your own path — the number on your card, the real app, the official site.
>
> That's how this scam works — so it never works on you.
