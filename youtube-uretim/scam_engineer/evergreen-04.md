> Whether you get money back from a scammer depends mostly on how you paid — and how fast you call. Here's what to do for each.

### [SAHNE 2 | Telefon santrali / sunucu odası (Wikimedia Commons) | 0:12–0:16]

### [SAHNE 3 | RECONSTRUCTION: Mutfak masası, banka uygulaması açık telefon, hediye kartı fişleri, yüz yok (Pexels). Köşede "Composite character" | Kesme: 3–4 sn | 0:16–0:45]
> Walter, seventy, realizes it on a Tuesday night. The "fraud department" that called him was the fraud. He sent two thousand dollars by Zelle, and bought five hundred dollars in gift cards.
>
> Is that money simply gone — or is there still something he can do tonight?
>
> There is. But the clock matters more than anything else — so pause this video if you need to, and come back after each call.

### [SAHNE 4 | Animasyon: "Payment rails": kart ağı (geri alma mekanizmalı), anlık banka transferi, havale, hediye kartı bakiyesi, blokzincir (kendi grafik) | 0:45–2:00]
> Here's the hidden system behind every payment: the rails it travels on. And each rail was designed with different rules for taking money back.
>
> Card networks were built with a dispute process. In the U.S., credit cards in particular come with legal rights to dispute charges.
>
> Instant bank transfers and payment apps were built for speed. Once money lands, reversing it usually depends on the bank or app agreeing to help.
>
> Wire transfers are designed to be final once they're collected.
>
> Gift cards are just a balance sitting on a company's system — and if the scammer hasn't spent it yet, the company may be able to freeze it.
>
> And cryptocurrency is designed so that nobody in the middle can reverse a payment. The FTC says that if you pay with crypto, you usually can't get it back unless the person you paid sends it back.
>
> That's also why scammers so often insist on gift cards, wires, payment apps or crypto, and steer people away from credit cards. They pick the rails that are hardest to reverse. When a caller is fussy about how you pay, that fussiness is the clue.
>
> So the first question is simple: how did you pay?

### [SAHNE 5 | Kart ikonu vurgulanıyor; kartın arka yüzü (kurgusal) | 2:00–2:55]
> **If you paid by credit or debit card:** call the company that issued the card, using the number on the back. Tell them it was a fraudulent charge and ask them to reverse it. Credit cards give you the strongest dispute rights. Debit cards come straight from your bank account, so call even faster.
>
> Ask them to cancel the card and send a new one, especially if you typed the number into a website or read it to a caller. Then check your statement for any other charges you don't recognize — once a card number is out, it's often tried more than once.

### [SAHNE 6 | Banka ve uygulama ikonları vurgulanıyor | 2:55–3:55]
> **If you paid by bank transfer, Zelle or another payment app:** call your bank or credit union immediately, and report it inside the app too. The FTC's guidance is to ask them to reverse the transaction.
>
> Be specific: say it was a scam, that you were tricked by someone pretending to be your bank or a company, give the date, the amount and the recipient's name or number. Ask what their fraud claim process is, and write down the claim number.
>
> Reversal isn't guaranteed with these instant payments. But banks can sometimes stop or recover money if they're told fast — and they can't try if they're never told.
>
> Walter's next call was about the gift cards. Is that a lost cause?

### [SAHNE 7 | Hediye kartı ikonu vurgulanıyor; kartın arka yüzü ve fiş (kurgusal) | 3:55–5:00]
> **If you paid with gift cards:** not necessarily lost. Call the company that issued the cards — the number is usually on the back — and tell them the cards were used in a scam. Ask whether the balance can be frozen or refunded.
>
> Keep the cards and the receipts. The FTC specifically advises holding on to both, because the card numbers and purchase details are how the company traces the money.
>
> And here's the thing worth repeating to everyone you love: no real government agency, bank or tech company will ever ask you to pay with gift cards. The moment someone asks, you know who's on the other end.
>
> **If you sent a wire transfer** through a company like Western Union or MoneyGram: call that company, say the transfer was fraudulent, and ask them to reverse it. If the money hasn't been picked up yet, there may still be time.
>
> **If you mailed cash,** the FTC says to contact the U.S. Postal Inspection Service and ask them to try to intercept the package. And if someone came to your home to collect money or gold, call your local police.

### [SAHNE 8 | Kripto ikonu; zincir bağlantıları (kendi grafik) | 5:00–6:10]
> **If you paid with cryptocurrency:** be realistic. Crypto payments are generally not reversible. Still, contact the exchange or app you used to send it, report the transaction details, and report the scam to the FTC and to the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center at ic3.gov. Occasionally, funds that land on a regulated exchange can be frozen.
>
> If the scam started on a website, a social network or a marketplace, report the account there too, so it can be taken down before it reaches the next person.
>
> Whatever the payment method, two reports help everyone: ReportFraud.ftc.gov, and ic3.gov. They rarely bring your money back on their own, but they help investigators connect cases.
>
> And now the most important warning in this video. After a scam, many people get a second call or ad — from a "recovery service," a "lawyer," or even someone claiming to be from a government agency — offering to get the money back for a fee. The FTC warns about exactly these refund and recovery scams.
>
> So how do you tell real help from a second scam?

### [SAHNE 9 | Walter'ın not defteri: arama listesi işaretleniyor ✓; sakin bir akşam | 6:10–7:20]
> Simple rule: real help doesn't charge you upfront to recover stolen money, and it doesn't ask you to pay with gift cards, crypto or wire transfers. Only use contacts you look up yourself — the number on your card, your bank's official app, the FTC and FBI websites.
>
> Here's what Walter did that night. He called his bank about the Zelle payment and opened a fraud claim. He called the gift card company with the cards and receipts in hand, and the unspent balance was frozen. He reported both at ReportFraud.ftc.gov and ic3.gov. And when a "recovery agent" called the next week, he hung up.
>
> Not every story ends with all the money back. But every one of these calls gives you a chance — and the chances shrink with every hour you wait.
>
> And if this happened to someone you love, the most useful thing you can do is sit with them and make those calls together tonight. Embarrassment costs time, and time is the one thing that matters most here.
>
> How you paid decides the path. How fast you call decides the odds.
>
> That's how this scam works — so it never works on you.
