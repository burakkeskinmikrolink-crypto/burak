# Scam Engineer #03: "Ödenmemiş otoyol ücreti" SMS'i aslında neyin peşinde?

- **Kanal:** Scam Engineer (@thescamengineer). *"A telecom engineer explains how scams really work, so they never work on you."*

- **Kitle:** ABD, 40+ yaş
- **Ton:** Sakin, bilgilendirici, güven veren. Korku müziği, "ACİL", "TEHLİKE" gibi ifadeler ve kırmızı alarm efektleri yok.

## Neden bu konu (not)
- 40+ ABD kitlesinin neredeyse herkesin telefonuna gelen bir mesaj. Yüksek tanınırlık ve yüksek arama hacmi var (toll text scam, E-ZPass scam text).
- Kanalın telekom açısına birebir uyuyor: SMS ile iMessage/RCS farkı, operatör filtresinin neden göremediği, "Y yaz" hilesi.
- Gerçek hedef ücret değil, **tek kullanımlık kodla kartın dolandırıcının telefonundaki dijital cüzdana eklenmesi**. Bu çoğu izleyicinin bilmediği bir bilgi, "aha" anı burada.
- Tatil seyahati döneminden önce yayınlanıyor.
- Önceki videolardan farkı: Spoofing videosu sesli aramayı, SIM swap videosu numaranın çalınmasını anlatıyordu. Bu video metin mesajı (smishing) ve dijital cüzdan üzerine.

## Senaryo

### [SAHNE 1 | Dikey telefon ekranı maketi: "Unpaid toll balance $12.51" mesajı (kendi tasarımın, gerçek kurum logosu yok) | 0:00–0:14]
> That unpaid toll text isn't after your twelve dollars — it's after a code that puts your card on a stranger's phone. By the end of this video, you'll know the one simple check that works on every text like it.

### [SAHNE 2 | Gerçek görsel: baz istasyonu kulesi (Wikimedia Commons: "Category:Cell towers") | 0:14–0:20]
> I'm a telecom engineer — I've spent my career inside the networks that carry your calls and texts.

### [SAHNE 3 | RECONSTRUCTION: Mutfak masası, kahve, kadın eli telefonu tutuyor, yüz görünmüyor (Pexels stok) | 0:20–0:52]
> Meet Diane. She's sixty-one, a retired teacher, and last week she drove to her sister's — across a toll road.
>
> On Tuesday morning, her phone buzzes. "Notice: you have an outstanding toll balance of $12.51. To avoid a late fee of $50, please pay by Friday."
>
> It sounds plausible. She did drive on a toll road. She taps the link. Nothing happens — the link isn't clickable.
>
> Then she notices the last line of the message: "Please reply Y, then exit and reopen this message to activate the link."
>
> She types Y.
>
> Now, pause right there. Because that one letter is the first clue. Why would a toll agency — or anyone — need you to reply before you can click a link?

### [SAHNE 4 | Animasyon: iki yol — "SMS → operatör ağı → filtre" ve "iMessage/RCS → internet → telefon" (kendi grafik) | 0:52–2:05]
> Here's what's happening under the hood.
>
> On an iPhone, Apple's Messages app disables links in texts from unknown senders. It's a safety feature. But if you reply — or save the sender as a contact — the links come back to life. Apple confirmed this behavior to the tech site BleepingComputer, after researchers spotted scam texts telling people to reply Y.
>
> There's a second reason they want a reply. It tells them your number is real, and that someone is reading. Live numbers are worth more to them.
>
> And there's a telecom detail most people never hear. A classic SMS text travels through your mobile carrier's network, where carriers run spam filters. But according to security journalist Brian Krebs, many of these scam messages are sent as iMessage or RCS chat messages — which travel over the internet, as data, and largely bypass the carrier's text-message filters.
>
> In other words, the message arrives through a side door that your carrier isn't watching.
>
> So where do millions of these messages come from — all at once, all nearly identical?

### [SAHNE 5 | RECONSTRUCTION: Diane'in ekranında resmi görünümlü ödeme sayfası (kurgusal "State Toll Services" tasarımı, gerçek logo yok) | 2:05–3:15]
> Back to Diane. The link opens a page that looks official: a state seal, a toll-road photo, her "invoice." She types in her card number, the expiration date and the security code.
>
> Then the page says: "For your security, enter the verification code we've sent to your phone."
>
> A text arrives — and this one is real. It's from her actual bank.
>
> Behind the page is a business. Researchers have linked a huge share of these campaigns to a China-based group known as the Smishing Triad. In November 2025, Google sued the operators behind a phishing kit called Lighthouse, which it says was used in these schemes. Google said the operation had harmed over a million victims in 120 countries, and estimated that between 12.7 million and 115 million U.S. credit cards may have been compromised.
>
> And the kit was sold like software. One security firm reported subscriptions ranging from 88 dollars a week to about 1,600 dollars a year.
>
> But wait. Diane only owed twelve dollars and fifty-one cents. Why would anyone need a code for that?

### [SAHNE 6 | Animasyon: kart → yabancı bir telefondaki dijital cüzdan → çok sayıda telefon bir kutuda (kendi grafik) | 3:15–4:30]
> Because the twelve dollars was never the point.
>
> When you add a card to Apple Pay or Google Wallet, your bank often sends a one-time code to confirm it's really you. Krebs reported that these phishing kits use your card details to start adding your card to a digital wallet — on a phone the criminals control. Your bank, doing exactly what it should, texts you a code. If you type that code into the fake page, you've just approved their phone.
>
> From then on, they can tap to pay with your card in stores, or online, without ever holding the plastic. Krebs reported that groups load several stolen cards onto a single phone, and then sell those phones in bulk.
>
> That's the real product. Not twelve dollars. A working copy of your card in someone else's pocket.
>
> So how long has this been going on — and how big has it become?

### [SAHNE 7 | Gerçek görsel: FBI ve FTC kamu bilgilendirme materyalleri (ABD hükümeti eserleri, kamu malı); ABD haritasında eyaletler tek tek yanıyor animasyonu | 4:30–5:40]
> The FBI first warned about this wave in April 2024. At that point, its Internet Crime Complaint Center had received over 2,000 complaints since early March, from at least three states — and said the scam appeared to be moving state to state. The sample message in that warning? A $12.51 balance and a $50 late fee.
>
> The Federal Trade Commission issued its own alerts, and its advice is blunt: if you get a text about unpaid tolls, it's probably a scam — whether you've driven through a toll recently or not.
>
> Since then, the same machinery has pivoted to other disguises: package deliveries, DMV notices, bank alerts. The costume changes. The method doesn't.
>
> And that's good news, because it means one check works on all of them. So what is it?

### [SAHNE 8 | Tablo grafiği: "Message → ✗" / "Your own path → ✓" ; telefonda tarayıcıya adres yazılıyor (Pexels stok) | 5:40–6:50]
> Here it is: never trust the path the message gives you. Choose your own path.
>
> If a text says you owe a toll, don't use its link, and don't reply — not even with a Y. Open your toll account by typing the agency's address yourself, or using the app you already have, or calling the number on your statement. If you really owe something, it'll be there.
>
> Second: read your codes. Many banks' verification texts say what the code is for. If a code mentions adding your card to a wallet, and you didn't ask for that, don't share it — with anyone, including a website.
>
> Third, on an iPhone, keep "Filter Unknown Senders" turned on in your Messages settings. It quietly moves these texts out of your main inbox.
>
> And report it. Forward the text to 7726 — that spells SPAM — or use your phone's "Report Junk" option. You can also report at ReportFraud.ftc.gov or ic3.gov. Then delete it.

### [SAHNE 9 | Sakin kapanış: Diane telefonu bırakıyor, banka kartının arkasındaki numarayı arıyor (Pexels stok, yüz görünmüyor) | 6:50–7:55]
> And if, like Diane, you already typed in your card or a code? Don't panic — act. Call your bank using the number on the back of your card. Tell them you entered your details on a fake site and may have shared a verification code. Ask them to cancel the card and remove any digital wallets it was added to. Then watch your statements, and report it.
>
> So, let's close our questions. Why did the text want a reply? To switch the links back on, and confirm a live number. Where do millions come from at once? Phishing kits sold like software, sending over internet messaging that sidesteps carrier filters. And why a code for twelve dollars? Because the code was the real prize.
>
> As an engineer, here's what I find reassuring: a text message is cheap to fake. But it can't force you to use its link. The moment you choose your own path, the whole machine stops working.
>
> That's how this scam works — so it never works on you.

## Shorts (3 adet, **üç farklı format**)

### Short 1: QUIZ (A/B/C) (35 sn)
- **Ekran:** Mesaj: "Reply Y to activate the link." Şıklar ekranda.
- **VO:** Quick quiz. A text about an unpaid toll says: "Reply Y to activate the link." Why do scammers want that Y? A) To confirm your payment. B) To switch iPhone's link protection back on and confirm your number is live. C) It's required by law. … The answer is B. iPhones disable links from unknown senders, and replying turns them back on. One letter, and the safety feature's gone. Don't reply. Don't tap. Delete and report to 7726.

### Short 2: KISA HİKÂYE (40 sn)
- **Görsel:** POV telefon ekranı, mesajlar tek tek geliyor.
- **VO:** Diane got a text: a twelve-dollar toll, a fifty-dollar late fee. She tapped, typed her card, and then the site asked for a code. A real code, from her real bank. Here's what she didn't know: that code wasn't for a toll. It was her bank asking, "Do you want to add this card to a new phone?" The scammers' phone. Twelve dollars was the bait. Her card was the catch. If a code mentions a wallet you didn't set up, never share it. *(Ekranda: "Composite character")*

### Short 3: GERÇEK Mİ SAHTE Mİ? (30 sn)
- **Ekran:** Üç mesaj sırayla. Her biri için "REAL or FAKE?" sorusu ve 2 sn bekleme.
- **VO:** Real or fake? One: "Unpaid toll, $6.99, pay today at this link." Fake. Toll agencies' bills are in your own account, not in random links. Two: "Your verification code is 482913. Don't share it with anyone." Real format. But if you didn't request it, someone else did. Three: "Reply Y to activate this link." Fake, every time. The rule: never use the path the message gives you. Pick your own.
