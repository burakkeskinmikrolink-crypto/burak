# Scam Engineer #05: Bir öğleden sonrada kurulan sahte mağaza

- **Kanal:** Scam Engineer (@thescamengineer)
- **Yayın:** 25 Kasım 2026 (Black Friday haftası; konular.md, 3. hafta)
- **Kitle:** ABD, 40+ yaş
- **Ton:** Sakin, bilgilendirici. Alışverişi kötülemiyor, "online alışveriş tehlikeli" demiyor. Mesaj "nasıl kontrol edersin".
- **Hedef süre:** ~8 dk

## Neden bu konu (not)
- FTC'nin Nisan 2026 verisine göre 2025'te sosyal medyada başlayan dolandırıcılıkların kaybı 2,1 milyar $ oldu (2020'nin ~8 katı). En çok bildirilen tür **alışveriş dolandırıcılığı**. Para kaybedenlerin %40'ından fazlası işin bir reklamdan sipariş vermekle başladığını söylüyor.
- Black Friday haftası, "%70 indirim" reklamlarının zirve yaptığı dönem.
- Telekom/internet mühendisi açısı: alan adı (domain) bir telefon numarası gibi dakikalar içinde kiralanabiliyor, kilit simgesi (HTTPS) ise sadece bağlantının şifreli olduğunu gösteriyor. Bunlar kanalın "altyapıyı bilen kişi" konumuna tam uyuyor.

> 🎬 **Dramatizasyon:** "Carol" **temsilî bir karakter**, FTC'nin tarif ettiği tipik vakalardan derlendi. Mağaza, marka ve ürün kurgusal. Gerçek marka logosu gösterme. Ekranda "Reconstruction – composite character" yaz.

## Senaryo

### [SAHNE 1 | Telefon ekranında parlak, kurgusal bir mağaza sayfası "70% OFF – Today Only" (kendi tasarımın) | 0:00–0:14]
> The store Carol bought from looked perfect — and its web address was eleven days old. By the end of this video, you'll know three quick checks that expose a fake shop before you pay.

### [SAHNE 2 | Gerçek görsel: fiber optik kablolar ve sunucu bağlantıları (Wikimedia Commons: "Category:Optical fiber") | 0:14–0:20]
> I'm a telecom engineer — I've spent my career inside the networks that carry your calls, texts and clicks.

### [SAHNE 3 | RECONSTRUCTION: Akşam, koltuk, tablette sosyal medya akışı; bir reklam beliriyor (kurgusal arayüz, yüz yok) | 0:20–0:52]
> Meet Carol. She's fifty-seven, and she's been looking for a particular pair of winter boots for her daughter. Two days before Thanksgiving, scrolling through social media, she sees them: the exact boots, seventy percent off. "Holiday clearance. Today only."
>
> She taps the ad. The website is beautiful. Professional photos. A countdown timer. Reviews with five gold stars. A little padlock in the address bar. Free shipping.
>
> She checks out with her debit card. A confirmation email arrives within seconds, with an order number and a cheerful "Thank you!"
>
> The boots never come.
>
> Here's the first question. How can a fake store look better than a real one?

### [SAHNE 4 | Animasyon: "Template + photos + domain + payment page = store" yapboz parçaları birleşiyor (kendi grafik) | 0:52–2:05]
> Because looking good is the cheapest part.
>
> Building an online store today takes no programming. Templates are everywhere. Product photos can be copied from a real brand's website. Reviews can be invented. A countdown timer is a few lines of code. And the FBI has warned that scammers now use AI tools to mimic legitimate websites and retailers even more convincingly.
>
> Then there's the address. In my world, a web address — a domain name — works a lot like a phone number. It's an entry in a global directory, the Domain Name System. And just like a phone number, anyone can rent one, in minutes, for a few dollars. When it gets reported, they rent another.
>
> Real retailers spend years building a reputation, a returns department and a customer service line. A fake shop skips all of that. It only needs to survive long enough to take orders — a few weeks around a big shopping holiday is plenty.
>
> So the scammer's costs are tiny, and their store can be ready in an afternoon. Carol's store wasn't hacked or broken. It was built to look exactly like this.
>
> But that leaves another question. Out of millions of shoppers, how did this store find Carol?

### [SAHNE 5 | Gerçek görsel: FTC Data Spotlight rakamları metin grafiği olarak (ABD hükümeti eseri); sosyal medya akışında reklam ikonları | 2:05–3:15]
> It paid to. In April 2026, the Federal Trade Commission published new data on scams that start on social media. In 2025, people reported losing 2.1 billion dollars to them — about eight times as much as in 2020.
>
> And the most reported type of scam on social media? Shopping. More than forty percent of people who lost money to a social media scam told the FTC it started when they ordered something they saw in an ad.
>
> The FTC says many of those ads led to unfamiliar websites. Others led to sites impersonating well-known brands, offering big discounts.
>
> Ads are built to reach the people most likely to click. If you've been searching for winter boots, you're exactly who a boot ad wants. Carol wasn't careless. She was targeted.
>
> So, where did Carol's money go — and what, if anything, was ever going to arrive?

### [SAHNE 6 | Pexels stok: boş kapı önü, ardından küçük bir kargo paketi içinden ucuz bir ürün çıkıyor | 3:15–4:30]
> According to the FTC, most people in these cases said their order simply never arrived. Others received something — but it was counterfeit, or very different from what was advertised, and often shipped from China.
>
> That second version is clever. A cheap item with a tracking number makes it harder to prove you were scammed. The package technically arrived.
>
> The FBI tracks a related category it calls non-payment and non-delivery: you pay, and you never get the goods. In its 2025 Internet Crime Report, it recorded over 56,000 complaints of this kind.
>
> And Carol paid with a debit card, which matters — because a debit card takes money straight out of your bank account, and getting it back can work differently than with a credit card. We'll come back to that.
>
> But first, the thing that reassured her most: that little padlock in the address bar. Doesn't the padlock mean a website is safe?

### [SAHNE 7 | Animasyon: Tarayıcı adres çubuğunda kilit simgesi; kilit sadece kullanıcı ile site arasındaki "boruyu" kaplıyor, sitenin kendisini değil (kendi grafik) | 4:30–5:40]
> This is the most common misunderstanding I hear, so let me explain it the way an engineer would.
>
> The padlock means your connection is encrypted — technically, it's using HTTPS. Nobody in the middle, on the Wi-Fi or the network, can read what you type. That's genuinely important.
>
> But encryption protects the pipe, not the person at the other end. Certificates that enable the padlock are available free to anyone who controls a domain — including scammers. So a fake shop can have a perfect padlock.
>
> Think of it like a phone call. A clear, private line tells you nobody is eavesdropping. It tells you nothing about whether the person you're talking to is honest.
>
> So if the padlock can't tell you, what can? Here are the three checks.

### [SAHNE 8 | Ekran kaydı tarzı: ICANN Lookup sayfası (lookup.icann.org) örnek sorgu; arama motorunda "store name + reviews" (kendi ekran kaydın) | 5:40–6:50]
> Check one: how old is the store? Go to lookup.icann.org, type in the website address, and look for the creation date. A store claiming decades of history with a web address registered a few weeks ago is a giant red flag.
>
> Check two: search for the store somewhere else. Don't trust the reviews on its own page. Type the store's name into a search engine with words like "reviews," "complaints" or "scam." The FTC recommends exactly this. Silence, or a wave of complaints, both tell you something.
>
> Check three: how does it want you to pay? If it pushes bank transfers, payment apps, gift cards or cryptocurrency — or offers a discount to avoid cards — stop. Use a credit card. Under the Fair Credit Billing Act, you can dispute a charge for goods you never received.
>
> And one bonus habit: if a deal reached you through an ad, close the ad and go to the brand's real website yourself. If the deal is real, it'll be there too.

### [SAHNE 9 | RECONSTRUCTION: Carol telefonda bankasını arıyor, sakin; ardından tablette FTC ihbar sayfası (yüz yok) | 6:50–7:55]
> If it's already happened, like it did to Carol? Act quickly, and calmly. Call your bank or card issuer using the number on your card, tell them the goods never arrived, and ask about disputing the charge. Credit cards give you the strongest legal rights; with debit cards, it depends on your bank's policies, so call right away. Then report the store at ReportFraud.ftc.gov and ic3.gov, and report the ad on the platform where you saw it.
>
> So, our questions. How can a fake store look better than a real one? Because looks are cheap, and templates and AI make them cheaper. How did it find Carol? Ads target the people most likely to click. Does the padlock mean safe? It means private — not honest.
>
> As an engineer, I find this hopeful. The scammer can fake the photos, the reviews and the padlock. What they can't fake is time. A web address can't pretend to be ten years old.
>
> That's how this scam works — so it never works on you.

## Shorts (3 adet, **üç farklı format**)

### Short 1: QUIZ (A/B/C) (35 sn)
- **Ekran:** Adres çubuğunda kilit simgesi. Soru: "The padlock means…"
- **VO:** Quick quiz. You see a little padlock next to a store's web address. What does it mean? A) The store is verified and safe. B) Your connection to the site is encrypted. C) The site has been checked by your bank. … The answer is B. The padlock protects the line, not the person at the other end. Scammers get padlocks for free. So check the store's age and reputation, not just the lock.

### Short 2: KISA HİKÂYE (40 sn)
- **Görsel:** POV: tablette reklam akışı, ardından mağaza, onay e-postası, boş kapı önü.
- **VO:** Carol saw the exact boots her daughter wanted. Seventy percent off. Today only. The site had photos, five-star reviews and a padlock. She paid. She got a "thank you" email in seconds. The boots never came. Later she looked up the web address. It had been registered eleven days earlier. Before you buy from an ad, look up the store's age at lookup.icann.org. A fake can copy everything except time. *(Ekranda: "Composite character")*

### Short 3: GERÇEK Mİ SAHTE Mİ? (30 sn)
- **Ekran:** Üç işaret sırayla, her biri için "RED FLAG or OK?" ve 2 sn bekleme.
- **VO:** Red flag or OK? One: the store offers ten percent off if you pay by bank transfer. Red flag. They're steering you away from card protection. Two: the web address was registered last month, but the store says it's "est. 1998." Red flag. Three: you found the brand's site by typing its name yourself, and it has the same deal. OK. That's the safe path. Pay by credit card, and never pay a shop that came to you through a surprise discount.
