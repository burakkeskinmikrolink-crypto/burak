# Scam Engineer #04: "Phantom Hacker": üç sahte kurtarıcı, tek bir birikim

- **Kanal:** Scam Engineer (@thescamengineer)
- **Yayın:** 18 Kasım 2026
- **Kitle:** ABD, 40+ yaş (ve yaşlı ebeveynleri olanlar)
- **Ton:** Sakin, saygılı, mağduru suçlamayan. "Aptallar kanar" imasını asla yapma.
- **Hedef süre:** ~7,5 dk (anlatım ~1.130 kelime, 150 kelime/dk; süreler yaklaşık)

## Neden bu konu (not)
- FBI'ın en büyük kayıplar arasında saydığı dolandırıcılık türlerinden biri. 2025'te 60 yaş üstünün bildirdiği toplam kayıp 7,7 milyar $ oldu (IC3 2025 raporu).
- 40+ kitlenin hem kendisi hem anne-babası için birebir uyuyor. İzleyicinin videoyu ebeveyniyle paylaşma ihtimali yüksek.
- Telekom açısı var: pop-up'taki telefon numarası, uzaktan erişim, sahte "banka" araması. Spoofing videosuna doğal bir gönderme yapılabiliyor ama konu tekrarı değil.
- Tatil öncesi aile ziyaretlerinde konuşulacak bir konu: "Bunu annene/babana göster."

> 🎬 **Dramatizasyon:** "Frank" **temsilî bir karakter**, FBI'ın tarif ettiği üç aşamalı senaryodan derlendi. Ekranda "Reconstruction – composite character" yaz.

## Senaryo

### [SAHNE 1 | Karanlık oda, dizüstü ekranında yanıp sönen kurgusal uyarı penceresi (kendi tasarımın, gerçek şirket logosu yok) | 0:00–0:13]
> In this scam, the hacker doesn't exist — and three fake rescuers take people's life savings anyway. By the end, you'll know the one sentence that stops this scam at any stage.

### [SAHNE 2 | Gerçek görsel: telefon santrali / fiber kablolar (Wikimedia Commons: "Category:Telephone exchanges") | 0:13–0:19]
> I'm a telecom engineer, and I've spent my career inside the networks these callers use.

### [SAHNE 3 | RECONSTRUCTION: Ev ofisi, yaşlı bir adamın elleri klavyede, yüz görünmüyor; ekranda alarm penceresi (Pexels stok + kendi grafik) | 0:19–0:52]
> Meet Frank. He's sixty-eight, a retired machinist, and he's careful with money. He's reading the news on his laptop when the screen freezes.
>
> A window fills the display. A loud tone. "Security alert: your computer has been compromised. Do not shut down. Call support immediately." And a phone number.
>
> Frank isn't reckless. He's worried. His whole retirement is online. So he calls.
>
> A calm, friendly man answers. He says he's from technical support. He asks Frank to install a small program, so he can "take a look."
>
> Hold on to this moment, because it's the first clue: a real security warning wants to fix a problem. Why would this one need you to call them?

### [SAHNE 4 | Animasyon: Aşama 1/3 — "Tech Support" etiketi; pop-up'ın aslında sıradan bir web sayfası olduğunu gösteren kod görüntüsü (kendi grafik) | 0:52–2:05]
> Here's what was actually on Frank's screen: a webpage. Just a webpage, designed to look like a system alert, often with sound, and sometimes coded to make the browser hard to close. It didn't scan anything. It can't.
>
> And the phone number? In my world, a phone number on a screen proves nothing about who answers it. Getting a number today can take minutes online.
>
> The FBI calls this the "Phantom Hacker" scam, and describes it in three phases. Phase one is the fake tech support agent. Once Frank installs that program, the caller can see and control his computer. He scrolls through ordinary system logs and calls them evidence. Then he asks Frank to log into his bank account, "to check for unauthorized charges."
>
> That's how he learns which bank Frank uses — and how much is there.
>
> "I'm sorry, sir," he says. "A foreign hacker has accessed your accounts. Your bank's fraud department will contact you."
>
> So now the second question: why would Frank believe the next call?

### [SAHNE 5 | Animasyon: Aşama 2/3 — "Bank" etiketi; telefonda gerçek görünen bir banka numarası beliriyor (kurgusal numara) | 2:05–3:15]
> Because it comes exactly as promised. A woman calls, says she's from his bank's fraud department. If you've watched my video on caller ID spoofing, you already know the number on his screen can look like the bank's real one.
>
> She confirms what the "technician" said. The hacker is still inside. And then she gives the instruction this whole scam is built around: to protect his savings, Frank must move them into a "safe" account — one the FBI says scammers often claim is connected to the Federal Reserve or another government agency.
>
> One more thing, she adds. "Don't tell the staff at your branch. We think someone there may be involved."
>
> And that's the most important line in the entire script. Because it raises our third question: why would a careful person believe his own bank teller might be in on it?

### [SAHNE 6 | Animasyon: Aşama 3/3 — "Government" etiketi; resmi görünümlü kurgusal antetli kâğıt (gerçek ajans mührü kullanma) | 3:15–4:30]
> Because each phase confirms the last one. The technician predicted the bank's call. The bank confirmed the technician. Two "institutions," agreeing with each other. It feels like the system is working.
>
> And then the warning about the branch staff does something clever: it cuts Frank off from the one person who might stop him. Isolation is the engine of this scam.
>
> Phase three arrives if there's doubt. Someone calls claiming to be from a U.S. government agency. The FBI says scammers in this scheme may even send letters on what looks like official government letterhead.
>
> In some versions, victims are told to withdraw cash or buy gold — and a courier comes to the house to collect it "for safekeeping." The FBI warned about this in early 2024, reporting over 55 million dollars in losses from May to December 2023 alone. An FBI field office later counted at least 1,737 such cases nationwide, with losses of about 186 million dollars, between 2023 and May 2025.
>
> So how big is this, really?

### [SAHNE 7 | Gerçek görsel: FBI IC3 yıllık raporundan rakamlar, metin grafiği olarak yeniden çizilmiş (ABD hükümeti eseri, kamu malı) | 4:30–5:40]
> When the FBI first named the Phantom Hacker scam in 2023, it said that in just the first half of that year, it received over 19,000 complaints about tech-support scams, with estimated losses over 542 million dollars. Nearly half of the victims were over sixty — and they accounted for about two-thirds of the losses.
>
> It hasn't slowed down. In its 2025 report, the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center said Americans aged sixty and over filed more than 201,000 complaints, reporting about 7.7 billion dollars in losses. That's a rise of nearly sixty percent from the year before.
>
> Those numbers aren't about foolish people. Many victims are exactly like Frank: careful, responsible, and trying to protect what they've built.
>
> Which brings us to the most useful question of all: where's the weak point — the place anyone can stop it?

### [SAHNE 8 | Sakin grafik: üç aşama yan yana, her birinin üstünde aynı cümle beliriyor | 5:40–6:50]
> Here are the facts that break each phase.
>
> Phase one: Microsoft says its real error and warning messages never include a phone number to call. If a pop-up gives you a number, close the browser, or restart the computer. And never let someone you don't know take control of your computer.
>
> Phase two: your bank won't ask you to move your money to a "safe account" to protect it. And the Federal Reserve doesn't hold accounts for individuals.
>
> Phase three: the FBI says the U.S. government will never ask you to send money by wire, cryptocurrency or gift cards — or to buy gold.
>
> But you don't need to memorize three lists. Here's the one sentence that works at every stage:
>
> "I'm going to hang up and call you back on a number I look up myself."
>
> Real banks, real companies, real agencies are fine with that. The scam isn't. It can only survive while you stay on their line. So if anyone tells you that hanging up is dangerous, or that you mustn't call anyone else, that tells you everything you need to know about who's really on the other end.

### [SAHNE 9 | RECONSTRUCTION: Frank telefonu kapatıyor, kartın arkasındaki numarayı arıyor; sonra mutfakta ailesiyle konuşuyor (Pexels stok, yüz görünmüyor) | 6:50–7:55]
> And if you, or someone you love, is already in the middle of it? It's not too late to act. Disconnect the computer from the internet. Call your bank using the number on your card or statement. Report it at ic3.gov. And if you need someone to talk it through with, the Justice Department runs a National Elder Fraud Hotline: 1-833-FRAUD-11.
>
> So, our questions. Why did the warning need you to call? Because the "alert" was just a webpage — the call was the trap. Why believe the bank's call? Because it was predicted, and the number can be faked. Why distrust the teller? Because isolation is what keeps the scam alive.
>
> And here's what I'd tell my own parents. The hacker in this story never existed. The only real connection was a phone line — and you're always allowed to hang up.
>
> That's how this scam works — so it never works on you.

## Shorts (3 adet, **üç farklı format**)

### Short 1: QUIZ (A/B/C) (35 sn)
- **Ekran:** "Your bank's fraud team says: move your savings to a SAFE account. What do you do?"
- **VO:** Your bank's "fraud department" calls. They say a hacker is in your account and you need to move your savings to a safe account today. What do you do? A) Move it, they're the bank. B) Ask them to stay on the line while you visit the branch. C) Hang up and call the number on the back of your card. … It's C. Real banks don't ask you to move money to protect it. And "stay on the line" is how they keep control.

### Short 2: KISA HİKÂYE (40 sn)
- **Görsel:** Dizüstü ekranı donuyor → telefon çalıyor → mektup zarfı.
- **VO:** Frank's laptop froze. A warning said "call support." He did. The technician found a "hacker" and said his bank would call. It did. The bank said move your savings to a safe account, and don't tell the branch staff. Then a "government agent" sent a letter. Three voices, all agreeing. None of them real. The FBI calls it the Phantom Hacker scam. The fix is one sentence: "I'll call you back on a number I look up myself." *(Ekranda: "Composite character")*

### Short 3: GERÇEK Mİ SAHTE Mİ? (30 sn)
- **Ekran:** Üç ifade, her biri için "REAL or FAKE?"
- **VO:** Real or fake? One: a pop-up says "Call Microsoft at this number." Fake. Microsoft says its real warnings never include a phone number. Two: a caller from the Federal Reserve offers to hold your savings safely. Fake. The Fed doesn't hold accounts for individuals. Three: your bank says, "Feel free to hang up and call us back." Real. Real institutions never mind you hanging up. Scammers do.
