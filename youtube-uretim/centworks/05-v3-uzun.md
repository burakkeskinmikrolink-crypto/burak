### [SAHNE 3 | RECONSTRUCTION: Mutfak masasında dizüstü, bir çift kredi teklifine bakıyor, yüz yok (Pexels) | Kesme: 3–4 sn | 0:16–0:45]
> Ana and Chris found the house. In early October, they asked for a rate. Freddie Mac's average that week: 7.40 percent. A year earlier: about 6.2.
>
> On their loan, that's roughly 280 dollars a month — for the same house, at the same price.
>
> "It's the Fed," Chris says. But the Fed doesn't set mortgage rates. So why did theirs jump?
>
> To answer that, we need to follow one interest rate through the whole economy.

*(Ekranda: "Illustrative example: $400,000, 30-year fixed, principal & interest only")*

### [SAHNE 4 | Marriner S. Eccles Federal Reserve binası (Wikimedia Commons); animasyon: Fed faizi → kısa vadeli faizler / 10 yıllık tahvil → mortgage | 0:44–1:54]
> The Fed controls one thing directly: a target for the federal funds rate, the interest rate banks charge each other for overnight loans. That's it. One short-term rate.
>
> On September 16th, 2026, the Fed raised that target by a quarter of a percentage point, to a range of 3.75 to 4 percent — its first increase since 2023. In late October, it held steady, but some officials signaled they were open to another hike.
>
> The reason was inflation. Prices in September were 3.7 percent higher than a year earlier, with energy costs doing a lot of the pushing.
>
> Mortgages don't follow the Fed directly. They mostly track long-term bond yields, like the ten-year Treasury, which move on what investors expect inflation and the Fed to do over years, not days. When markets started to expect more hikes, long-term rates rose, and mortgage rates rose with them.
>
> So for Ana and Chris, the Fed mattered — just indirectly. But how does a committee in Washington change a number at your bank in the first place?

### [SAHNE 5 | YENİ | Animasyon: masa etrafında 12 sandalye (FOMC, yüz yok) → takvimde 8 toplantı işareti → "IORB" musluğu bir su borusuna (gecelik faiz) akıyor; altta "ON RRP" tabanı; sonra boru dallanıyor: "PRIME = top of range + 3" → kart, HELOC; ayrı bir boru "10-YEAR TREASURY" → mortgage | 1:54–3:50]
> The Fed's rate-setting body, the Federal Open Market Committee, meets eight times a year. At each meeting, it picks a target range for the federal funds rate: the rate banks charge each other to borrow overnight.
>
> But the Fed doesn't phone every bank and order a new price. It changes what it pays. Banks keep reserves at the Federal Reserve, and the Fed pays interest on those reserve balances. If the Fed pays, say, four percent on money parked safely with it, no bank will lend that money to another bank overnight for much less. So the market rate follows.
>
> There's a floor, too, for big cash investors like money market funds, which can't earn that interest directly. They can lend to the Fed overnight through what's called the reverse repo facility. Between the two, the overnight rate stays inside the Fed's target range.
>
> So how does one overnight rate reach your credit card?
>
> Through the prime rate. Banks set prime — the rate for their most creditworthy customers — usually three percentage points above the top of the Fed's range. Credit cards and home-equity lines are priced as prime plus a margin. So when the range moves, prime moves the same day, and your card follows within a billing cycle or two. Savings accounts and CDs respond too, though more slowly, and not always fully.
>
> Long-term rates play by different rules. Ten-year Treasury yields, and the mortgages that follow them, respond to expectations: where investors think rates and inflation will be over years. That's why a single Fed meeting can move mortgage rates before it changes anything else — or barely move them at all.
>
> Now, the real what-if: if the Fed keeps going, what happens to your money?

### [SAHNE 6 | Ekranda üç dal: "HIKE" (kırmızı), "HOLD" (sarı), "CUT" (yeşil). İlk dal parlıyor. Animasyon: "Fed funds + 3 = Prime" → "Prime + margin = your APR" | 3:50–5:00]
> Branch one: the Fed raises rates again.
>
> Start with the debt that reacts fastest: your credit card. Most American credit cards have a variable rate — the prime rate plus a margin. And the prime rate typically moves in lockstep with the Fed. So when the Fed raises rates by a quarter point, most card APRs go up by about a quarter point within a billing cycle or two.
>
> Here's where the math surprises people. On a 6,000-dollar balance, a quarter-point hike adds about fifteen dollars a year in interest. Three more hikes? About forty-five.
>
> That's not nothing. But look at the level, not the change. According to Federal Reserve data, the average card rate across accounts was around 21 percent this year. On 6,000 dollars, that's roughly 1,270 dollars a year in interest — already.
>
> In this branch, new mortgages likely stay expensive, and savers with high-yield accounts usually see their rates nudge up.
>
> So more hikes make card debt worse. But the real damage was done long before September. Which leads to branch two.

*(Ekranda: "Illustrative example: $6,000 balance, held all year")*

### [SAHNE 7 | İkinci dal "HOLD" parlıyor; Pexels stok: kumbara, banka uygulaması ekranı (marka yok) | 5:00–6:31]
> Branch two: the Fed holds where it is.
>
> If you have a fixed-rate mortgage, nothing changes. That's the whole point of fixed. If you have a card balance, your rate stays high — around that 21 percent average — so the clock keeps ticking.
>
> So who comes out ahead if rates simply sit still?
>
> For savers, this branch is quietly important. When the Fed's rate is high, online high-yield savings accounts and certificates of deposit usually pay more. But two catches apply.
>
> A certificate of deposit can lock in today's rate for a fixed term, which helps if rates later fall. The trade-off: you usually pay a penalty if you need the money early.
>
> First, not every bank passes it on. Many big banks pay far less than the Fed's rate on standard savings accounts. If you've never compared, check what yours actually pays.
>
> Second, inflation. If prices rise 3.7 percent a year and your savings earn 4, your real gain — what your money can actually buy — is close to zero.
>
> There's a simple test: find the interest rate on your savings account, then compare it with the latest inflation figure. If the first number is smaller, your savings are losing buying power every month — even though the balance looks like it's growing.
>
> So savers do better than borrowers here. But better isn't always winning.

### [SAHNE 8 | Üçüncü dal "CUT" parlıyor; Freddie Mac 1971–2026 mortgage faizi grafiği, 1981 zirvesi işaretli (Freddie Mac verisinden kendi grafik) | 6:31–7:49]
> Branch three: what if the Fed eventually cuts?
>
> Cuts usually make borrowing cheaper, and can lower mortgage rates over time. Card APRs would drift down a little, and savings rates would usually follow them down. For Ana and Chris, a cut might eventually mean refinancing to a lower rate — though nobody can promise when. But the Fed only cuts when it believes inflation is under control. Cut too early, and prices can take off again.
>
> How bad can that get? That fear has a history. In the late 1970s, inflation spiralled. To break it, the Fed pushed short-term rates to around 20 percent in 1981. That October, Freddie Mac's average thirty-year mortgage rate hit 18.63 percent. Inflation did come down — at the price of a deep recession.
>
> Nobody is predicting a repeat. But it explains why central banks would rather move too firmly than too softly.
>
> And right now, the minutes of the September meeting showed most officials saw a need for at least one more hike this year. Markets were expecting a December move, though forecasts keep shifting with every inflation report.
>
> So does a run of rate hikes always end in a recession?

### [SAHNE 9 | YENİ | Zaman çizelgesi: "FEB 1994" → "FEB 1995"; fed funds çizgisi 3%'ten 6%'ya basamak basamak çıkıyor (7 basamak); yanında mortgage çizgisi 7%'den 9%'a; sonra 1995'te üç küçük iniş; büyüme çizgisi kesintisiz devam ediyor (Freddie Mac ve FRED verisinden kendi grafik) | 7:49–9:15]
> History has at least one famous counterexample.
>
> In February 1994, the Fed began raising rates. Over the following year, it raised them seven times and roughly doubled the federal funds rate, from three percent to around six.
>
> Borrowers felt it. Freddie Mac's thirty-year mortgage rate started that year a little above seven percent and finished it above nine. For a new buyer, that's hundreds of dollars more a month, for the same house.
>
> Bond investors felt it too. When new bonds start paying more, older bonds paying less become worth less, and long-term bond prices fell sharply that year.
>
> And yet there was no recession. The economy kept growing, inflation stayed under control, and in 1995 the Fed cut rates three times. Economists often call it the textbook soft landing. Alan Blinder, a former vice chair of the Fed, called it a perfect one.
>
> Two lessons for your money. First, rising rates and a recession are not the same thing. Second, rate cycles turn. The people hurt most in 1994 were those who had to borrow at the peak with no room to wait, or who had to sell long-term bonds in a hurry.
>
> So if a soft landing is possible, why does it take the Fed so long to know whether it has worked?

### [SAHNE 10 | YENİ | Animasyon: bir domino dizisi yavaşça devriliyor: "RATES" → "HOUSING" → "BUSINESS INVESTMENT" → "HIRING" → "PRICES"; üstünde bir saat "18–24 MONTHS"; sonra mutfak masasında bir zarf "EMERGENCY FUND" | 9:15–10:52]
> Because rate hikes work with what economists call "long and variable lags", a phrase that goes back to the economist Milton Friedman.
>
> A hike reaches some things almost immediately: card APRs, savings rates, and expectations in the bond market. But spending, hiring and prices adjust slowly. In 2022, the president of the Atlanta Fed, Raphael Bostic, wrote that a large body of research suggests tighter policy can take eighteen months to two years or more to have its full effect on inflation.
>
> The housing market usually feels it first, because it runs on borrowing. Then businesses delay investments, and hiring slows.
>
> So what does that mean for jobs?
>
> It's the part of a rate hike that rarely makes the headline. Cooling the economy is the goal, and a cooler economy usually means fewer job openings and slower raises. The Fed is trying to slow things just enough, without tipping into a downturn.
>
> For your household, that changes the order of priorities. The first defence against a slowdown isn't squeezing out the best savings rate. It's an emergency fund you can actually reach, and debt payments you could keep making if your income dipped for a few months.
>
> It also means today's headline doesn't tell you where the economy will be when it matters. The decisions made this autumn will still be working their way through the system next year, and the year after.
>
> Which brings us back to the three branches.

### [SAHNE 11 | Kontrol listesi grafiği; sakin, pozitif renkler | 10:52–11:58]
> So which branch will it be? Nobody knows. But here's what's true in all three.
>
> If you already have a fixed-rate mortgage, a Fed decision doesn't change your payment.
>
> If you have variable-rate debt — a credit card, a home-equity line, an adjustable-rate mortgage — that's the debt the Fed reaches fastest. Paying it down is the one move that helps in every branch.
>
> If you have an emergency fund, keep it somewhere safe and easy to reach. That matters more than squeezing out the last tenth of a percent.
>
> If you have savings, compare rates. FDIC-insured high-yield accounts and CDs often pay far more than a standard account.
>
> And if you're shopping for a home, remember the house price and the rate are two different decisions. You can't control the Fed. You can control your down payment, your credit score, and how many lenders you compare.
>
> Don't let a scary headline rush you into anything. Rates move week to week. A good plan shouldn't have to.

### [SAHNE 12 | Ana ve Chris'in masası; teklif kâğıdı, kalem; ekranda üç dal yan yana, ortadaki ortak madde "Pay down variable debt" parlıyor | 11:58–12:50]
> Back to Ana and Chris. Their rate jumped because mortgages follow long-term bond yields, and those rose as markets expected more Fed hikes. The Fed didn't set their rate. It set the mood.
>
> And the real what-if? If the Fed keeps going, borrowing gets more expensive and the economy slows — because that's the intention. The Fed raises rates to cool things down. The only question is how much cooling it takes.
>
> Whichever branch we end up on, the moves that protect your wallet are the same: keep fixed debt fixed, shrink variable debt, and make your savings earn their keep.
>
