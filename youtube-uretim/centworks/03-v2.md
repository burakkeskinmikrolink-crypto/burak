### [SAHNE 3 | RECONSTRUCTION: Benzin istasyonu marketi, kasa çekmecesinde boş kuruş bölmesi (Pexels: "cash register drawer") | Kesme: 3–4 sn | 0:15–0:45]
> A customer's total is four ninety-three. He hands over a five. The cashier opens the drawer to give back seven cents.
>
> The penny slot is empty.
>
> After the U.S. Mint stopped making pennies for circulation, stores in parts of the country said they couldn't get enough.
>
> So why did they run out, when hundreds of billions of pennies still exist?
>
> Some stores simply started rounding. One Midwest chain, Kwik Star, rounded cash totals down — in the customer's favor.

### [SAHNE 4 | Philadelphia Darphanesi (Wikimedia Commons: "United States Mint Philadelphia"); ABD Darphanesi'nin kamuya açık sent görselleri | 0:45–2:00]
> Estimates say somewhere between 250 and 300 billion pennies are still out there. To understand the shortage, rewind to November 12th, 2025, at the Philadelphia Mint. The Treasurer of the United States struck the final one-cent coins for circulation, ending a run of 232 years. The very last five carry a tiny omega mark, and are expected to be auctioned.
>
> The reason was simple arithmetic. According to the Mint, the cost of making a penny had climbed from 1.42 cents to 3.69 cents over a decade. Every new penny lost the government money.
>
> But stopping production didn't remove pennies from the economy. It changed how they move. Banks order coins through the Federal Reserve, and the Fed's network moves them around the country. When production ended, the Fed changed how it handled penny orders and deposits. Shortages followed in some regions, and in January 2026 the Fed reversed course and began accepting penny deposits again.
>
> Meanwhile, in September 2026, both chambers of Congress passed the Common Cents Act. It formally ends penny production and lets businesses round cash transactions to the nearest five cents.
>
> And that brings us to the question everyone actually cares about: when a store rounds, who wins — you, or them?

### [SAHNE 5 | Animasyon: Sayı doğrusu .00–.10; 1–2 aşağı, 3–4 yukarı, 6–7 aşağı, 8–9 yukarı okları | 2:00–3:15]
> Here's how the rounding works. The final cash total, after tax, goes to the nearest nickel. If it ends in one or two cents, it rounds down. Three or four, up to five. Six or seven, back down to five. Eight or nine, up to the next ten.
>
> So on any single purchase, you can lose up to two cents, or gain up to two cents. A coffee at three dollars and twelve cents becomes three ten. A sandwich at six forty-eight becomes six fifty. Over a year of cash purchases, those pennies mostly wash out.
>
> A lot of people assume this is a hidden tax. Economists at the Federal Reserve Bank of Richmond ran the numbers. Their estimate? Rounding without the penny would cost American consumers about six million dollars a year — in total. Spread across the whole country, that's a rounding error on a rounding error.
>
> Why so small? Because the ups and downs mostly cancel out, and because only cash transactions get rounded.
>
> But that last detail hides a twist. Can a store simply refuse your pennies — or your cash altogether?

### [SAHNE 6 | Federal Reserve binası (Wikimedia Commons: "Marriner S. Eccles Federal Reserve Board Building"); "LEGAL TENDER" metin grafiği | 3:15–4:25]
> The answer surprises people. U.S. coins and bills are legal tender for debts. But according to the Federal Reserve, there's no federal law that forces a private business to accept cash or coins in the first place. A store can set its own payment policy, unless a state or local law says otherwise.
>
> That's why rounding rules matter. They don't change the price of anything on the shelf. They change what happens at the very last step, when physical money meets a total that coins can no longer match.
>
> According to CNBC, by August 2026, twenty states had passed their own laws allowing or requiring cash rounding. California's version takes effect in July 2027. So depending on where you live, the rules at the register may already differ from the next state over.
>
> Now, if the penny was too expensive — what about the nickel?

### [SAHNE 7 | ABD Darphanesi kamu görseli: Jefferson nickel; üzerine "13.78¢" etiketi | 4:25–5:35]
> The nickel has the same problem, only bigger. In fiscal year 2024, the Mint reported it cost about 13.78 cents to make one five-cent coin. That's nearly three times its face value.
>
> The Common Cents Act doesn't kill the nickel. Instead, it lets the Mint switch to a cheaper metal mix — if testing shows the new coin wouldn't seriously disrupt vending machines and other coin-operated equipment.
>
> The Richmond Fed also modeled what would happen if the nickel disappeared too, with cash rounded to the nearest dime. Their estimate jumps to about 55.6 million dollars a year in extra cost to consumers — roughly nine times the penny-only figure.
>
> So the penny was the easy one. The nickel is where it starts to cost real money.
>
> But there's one more piece. Why does your card still pay four ninety-three — exactly?

### [SAHNE 8 | Aynı market, temassız kart ödemesi; ekranda "$4.93" (Pexels) | 5:35–6:45]
> Because rounding only applies to cash. Card payments, phone payments and other electronic transactions still charge you to the exact cent. The digital penny never died. Only the metal one did.
>
> That creates something new in America: two prices for the same basket. Pay with a card, and you pay four ninety-three. Pay cash, and you pay four ninety-five.
>
> Other countries have been here. Canada stopped distributing pennies in 2013, and rounded cash totals to the nearest five cents while card payments stayed exact. Life carried on.
>
> Some stores may even prefer to nudge customers toward cards, or simply set prices that already end in zero or five, so the rounding never comes up. Watch the price tags at your local shops over the next year — you may see more of them.
>
> The stakes for shoppers are tiny — a couple of cents either way. The bigger shift is cultural. For two centuries, an American price tag was a promise of the exact amount you'd hand over. For cash, it's now a close estimate.

### [SAHNE 9 | Bozuk para dolu kavanoz (Pixabay: "coin jar"); bankada bozuk para sayma makinesi | 6:45–7:45]
> So, nearly a year after the last penny was struck, here's what all of this means in practice.
>
> Stores ran out not because pennies vanished, but because most of them are sitting in jars and drawers, and the system that moved them changed. Rounding, on average, barely costs anyone: about six million dollars a year across the whole country. And at the federal level, a store can refuse cash unless your state or city says otherwise.
>
> So the practical move is the jar. If you've got one at home, it's still real money. Roll the coins, or take them to a bank or a coin counter, and turn them into something the system can use. Those pennies aren't worthless. They just stopped moving.
>
