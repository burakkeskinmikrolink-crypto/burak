## Senaryo

### [SAHNE 1 | Yakın plan: uçak kanadı penceresi (Pexels: "airplane window wing") → market kasası | 0:00–0:14]
> Every time you pay with cash or a basic debit card, you may be helping fund someone else's free flight. By the end, you'll know who really pays for card rewards — and which side you're on.

### [SAHNE 2 | Masa üstü: Fed raporları çıktısı, işaretleyici kalem (Pexels stok) | 0:14–0:19]
> I spend my evenings reading Federal Reserve research so you don't have to.

### [SAHNE 3 | RECONSTRUCTION: Market kasası, iki ayrı alışveriş arabası, aynı ürünler (Pexels stok: "grocery checkout") | 0:19–0:50]
> Two shoppers are standing in the same checkout line. Same groceries. Same total: one hundred dollars.
>
> The first one taps a premium rewards credit card. Somewhere in the background, two dollars in points land in her account. Next summer, that's part of a plane ticket.
>
> The second one pays cash.
>
> They paid the same price. They walked out with the same bags. But only one of them got two dollars back.
>
> So here's the question that sounds simple and isn't: the store charged them both exactly the same — so where did that two dollars actually come from?

*(Ekranda: "Illustrative example")*

### [SAHNE 4 | Animasyon: Kart → işyeri → ödeme ağı → kartı veren banka; okların üstünde ücret kesintileri (kendi grafik) | 0:50–2:05]
> To answer that, we need to follow the money through a system you never see.
>
> When you pay by card, the store doesn't receive the full hundred dollars. It pays a fee — often called the swipe fee. The biggest slice of that fee is called interchange, and it goes to the bank that issued your card.
>
> That's the fuel tank for rewards. The fancier the card, the more the bank typically collects in interchange — and the more it can hand back to you in points, miles and cash back.
>
> Think of it like a toll road. Your purchase travels from the store, through a card network like Visa or Mastercard, to your bank. Everyone along the route takes a small cut — and the store is the one paying the tolls. The customer at the register never sees the toll booth.
>
> Now, from the store's point of view, that fee is a cost of doing business, like rent or electricity. And like rent or electricity, it gets built into prices. Not into your price specifically — into everyone's.
>
> So when the rewards customer and the cash customer pay the same hundred dollars, they're both paying shelf prices that already include the store's card costs. One of them gets a slice back. The other doesn't.
>
> Which raises our second question: if debit cards also run on the same networks, why don't they pay rewards like credit cards do?

### [SAHNE 5 | Gerçek görsel: ABD Kongre binası (Wikimedia Commons: "United States Capitol") → "Regulation II" metin grafiği | 2:05–3:15]
> The answer is a law. In 2010, Congress passed the Dodd-Frank Act, and inside it was something called the Durbin Amendment. The Federal Reserve used it to cap debit interchange fees for large banks. Since 2011, the cap has been 21 cents plus 0.05% of the purchase, with a small extra allowance for fraud prevention.
>
> On a hundred-dollar purchase, that's only around a quarter. Not much room for generous rewards. That's a big reason debit cards from big banks rarely earn meaningful rewards — while credit cards, which have no such federal cap, compete on them.
>
> So the system rewards one kind of plastic, and not another. And that brings us to the uncomfortable part.
>
> Because if the money for rewards comes from prices everyone pays — then who is actually on the losing side?

### [SAHNE 6 | Gerçek görsel: Boston Federal Rezerv binası (Wikimedia Commons: "Federal Reserve Bank of Boston") → grafik: "$149" ve "$1,133" çubukları | 3:15–4:30]
> In 2010, economists at the Federal Reserve Bank of Boston tried to measure it. Their model estimated that, on average, each household paying by cash was transferring about 149 dollars a year to card users — and each card-using household was receiving about 1,133 dollars a year from cash users.
>
> They also looked at income. After rewards, the lowest-income households paid about 21 dollars a year, while the highest-income households received about 750.
>
> More recent research adds a twist. A team of economists studying Federal Reserve credit card data estimated an annual cross-subsidy of about 15.5 billion dollars tied to rewards. In their findings, the biggest losers weren't just lower-income people. They were people who carry a balance and pay interest — including some high earners. And the biggest winners were people who pay in full every month.
>
> So it's not only rich versus poor. It's people who use cards carefully, versus everyone else. If you've seen our video on why card companies like it when you pay late, this is the other half of that story: interest from some customers helps make the rewards for others possible.
>
> And now, something is about to change. Could a store soon turn down your rewards card?

### [SAHNE 7 | Gerçek görsel: Brooklyn federal adliyesi (Wikimedia Commons: "Theodore Roosevelt United States Courthouse") → uzlaşma maddeleri metin grafiği | 4:30–5:50]
> For nearly two decades, merchants have fought Visa and Mastercard in court over swipe fees. In June 2026, a federal judge in Brooklyn granted preliminary approval to a revised settlement — estimated to be worth around 38 billion dollars in savings for merchants over time.
>
> Here's what's in it. Interchange rates would come down by a tenth of a percentage point for five years. Rates on standard consumer credit cards would be capped at 1.25%. Merchants would get more freedom to add surcharges for credit card payments.
>
> And the big one: merchants could choose whether to accept different categories of cards — for example, accepting standard cards, but declining premium rewards cards.
>
> That's a crack in a rule called "Honor All Cards," which for years meant that if a store took one Visa credit card, it had to take them all.
>
> Not everyone is happy. Major retail groups have opposed the deal, and some merchants call the fee cut tiny. A final decision is still pending.

### [SAHNE 8 | Kasa ekranında "Premium cards not accepted" yazan kurgusal tabela (kendi grafik); market fişinde "Card surcharge" satırı | 5:50–6:55]
> So what could this mean at the register?
>
> If the deal is approved, you might see more stores adding a surcharge line for credit cards. You might, occasionally, see a sign saying a store doesn't take premium cards. Or nothing may change at all — because many merchants worry about losing customers who expect to pay with their favorite card.
>
> Nobody knows yet how many stores will actually use these new options. And it's worth being honest: the settlement doesn't send anyone a check. The 38 billion is an estimate of reduced fees, not cash in your pocket.
>
> Which leaves the last question — the personal one. If rewards are partly paid for by everyone else, should you stop using them?

### [SAHNE 9 | Cüzdan, tek bir kart ve bir hesap makinesi (Pexels stok) | 6:55–8:00]
> Let's answer everything.
>
> Where did the two dollars come from? From swipe fees the store pays — which are built into prices for every shopper in that line.
>
> Why don't debit cards pay much? Because federal rules cap debit fees at big banks.
>
> Who loses? Research suggests cash users, and people who carry balances and pay interest. Who wins? People who pay their card in full every month.
>
> So here's the practical takeaway. Refusing rewards won't lower your grocery bill — shelf prices are the same either way. If you can pay in full, every month, without overspending, a rewards card puts some of that hidden cost back in your pocket. If you carry a balance, the interest will almost certainly cost you more than any points you earn — and you're the one funding everyone else's flights.
>
> The system isn't hidden because it's secret. It's hidden because it's in every price.
