## Neden bu konu (not)
Fed 16 Eylül 2026'da 2023'ten beri ilk kez faiz artırdı ve politika faizini %3,75–4,00'e çıkardı. 28 Ekim'deki toplantıda faizi sabit tuttu ama iki üye yeni bir artış için muhalefet şerhi koydu. Eylül TÜFE'si yıllık %3,7 oldu, benzinde sert bir yükseliş var. 30 yıllık mortgage faizi 8 Ekim'de %7,40 ile zirve yaptı. Aralık toplantısından önce "ya artırmaya devam ederse?" sorusu tam zamanında.

## Senaryo

### [SAHNE 1 | Yakın plan: anahtar teslimi, "SOLD" tabelası (Pexels stok) → üzerine "+$284/month" yazısı | 0:00–0:14]
> The same four-hundred-thousand-dollar mortgage now costs about 280 dollars more a month than it did a year ago. By the end, you'll know what another Fed hike would do to your mortgage, your card and your savings.

### [SAHNE 2 | Masa üstü: FOMC açıklaması çıktısı, işaretleyici kalem (Pexels stok) | 0:14–0:19]
> I spend my evenings reading Federal Reserve research so you don't have to.

### [SAHNE 3 | RECONSTRUCTION: Mutfak masasında dizüstü, bir çift kredi teklifine bakıyor, yüzler görünmüyor (Pexels stok) | 0:19–0:52]
> Meet Ana and Chris. They've found the house. Three bedrooms, a yard, a school down the road. In early October, they ask their lender for a rate.
>
> That week, Freddie Mac's survey puts the average thirty-year fixed rate at 7.40 percent. A year earlier, it was about 6.2.
>
> On a four-hundred-thousand-dollar loan, that difference is roughly 280 dollars a month — more than three thousand dollars a year — for the exact same house, at the exact same price.
>
> Chris says what a lot of people said this autumn: "It's because the Fed raised rates."
>
> And he's partly right. But here's the strange thing: the Federal Reserve doesn't actually set mortgage rates. So why did theirs jump?

*(Ekranda: "Illustrative example: $400,000, 30-year fixed, principal & interest only")*

### [SAHNE 4 | Gerçek görsel: Marriner S. Eccles Federal Reserve binası (Wikimedia Commons); animasyon: Fed faizi → kısa vadeli faizler / 10 yıllık tahvil → mortgage | 0:52–2:05]
> Let's start with what the Fed really controls. It sets a target for the federal funds rate: the interest rate banks charge each other for overnight loans. That's it. One short-term rate.
>
> On September 16th, 2026, the Fed raised that target by a quarter of a percentage point, to a range of 3.75 to 4 percent. It was its first increase since 2023, and the vote was unanimous. In late October, it held steady — but two officials voted for another hike.
>
> Why? Inflation. The September consumer price index rose 3.7 percent from a year earlier, and 0.6 percent in a single month, as gasoline prices surged after the US and Israeli strikes on Iran.
>
> Mortgages don't follow the Fed directly. They mostly track long-term bond yields, like the ten-year Treasury — which move on what investors expect inflation and the Fed to do over years, not days. When markets started to expect more hikes, long-term rates rose, and mortgage rates rose with them.
>
> So for Ana and Chris, the Fed mattered — but indirectly. Now let's look at the debt where it matters directly: what happens to your credit card?

### [SAHNE 5 | Animasyon: "Fed funds + 3 = Prime" → "Prime + margin = your APR"; kart ekstresi grafiği | 2:05–3:15]
> Most credit cards in America have a variable interest rate. Your card's APR is usually the prime rate plus a margin. And the prime rate typically moves in lockstep with the Fed.
>
> So when the Fed raised rates by a quarter point, most variable card APRs went up by about a quarter point too — usually within a billing cycle or two.
>
> Here's where the "what if" math surprises people. Say you carry a 6,000-dollar balance. A quarter-point hike adds about fifteen dollars a year in interest. Three more hikes? About forty-five dollars.
>
> That's not nothing. But look at the level, not the change. According to Federal Reserve data, the average card rate across accounts was around 21 percent this year. On 6,000 dollars, that's roughly 1,270 dollars a year in interest — already.
>
> So more Fed hikes make card debt worse. But the real damage was done long before September.
>
> Now, if borrowers lose when rates rise, do savers finally win?

*(Ekranda: "Illustrative example: $6,000 balance, held all year")*

### [SAHNE 6 | Pexels stok: kumbara, banka uygulaması ekranı (marka yok) | 3:15–4:30]
> In theory, yes. When the Fed raises its rate, banks earn more on their own cash, and online high-yield savings accounts and certificates of deposit usually raise what they pay.
>
> But there are two catches.
>
> First, not every bank passes it on. Many big banks pay far less than the Fed's rate on standard savings accounts. If you've never compared, it's worth checking what your account actually pays.
>
> Second: inflation. If prices are rising 3.7 percent a year and your savings earn 4 percent, your real gain — what your money can actually buy — is close to zero. Earn less than inflation, and your savings are quietly shrinking.
>
> So savers do better than borrowers. But "better" isn't always "winning."
>
> Which brings us to the real what-if. If the Fed keeps going — what is it actually trying to prevent?

### [SAHNE 7 | Arşiv: 1970'ler/80'ler benzin kuyruğu (Library of Congress / National Archives kamu malı fotoğrafı); grafik: Freddie Mac 1971–2026 mortgage faizi, 1981 zirvesi işaretli | 4:30–5:40]
> The Fed is walking a tightrope. Raise rates too much, and borrowing gets so expensive that home sales stall, businesses cut back, and people lose jobs. Raise too little, and inflation can settle in — and once people expect prices to keep rising, it's much harder to stop.
>
> That second fear has a history. In the 1970s, inflation spiralled. To break it, the Fed under Paul Volcker pushed short-term rates to around 20 percent in 1981. That October, Freddie Mac's average thirty-year mortgage rate hit 18.63 percent. Inflation did come down — at the price of a deep recession.
>
> Nobody is predicting a repeat of that. But it explains why central banks would rather overreact than underreact.
>
> And right now? The minutes of the September meeting showed most officials saw a need for at least one more hike this year. Markets were expecting a December move, though the forecast keeps shifting with every inflation report.
>
> So, out of all this — what can you actually control?

### [SAHNE 8 | Kontrol listesi grafiği; sakin, pozitif renkler | 5:40–6:50]
> Quite a lot, as it happens.
>
> If you already have a fixed-rate mortgage, a Fed hike doesn't change your payment at all. That's the point of fixed.
>
> If you have variable-rate debt — a credit card, a home-equity line, an adjustable-rate mortgage — that's the debt the Fed reaches fastest. Paying it down is the one move that helps in every scenario: hikes, holds or cuts.
>
> If you have savings, compare rates. FDIC-insured high-yield accounts and CDs often pay far more than a standard account.
>
> And if you're shopping for a home, remember the house price and the rate are two different decisions. You can't control the Fed. You can control your down payment, your credit score, and how many lenders you compare.
>
> And don't let a scary headline about the Fed rush you into anything. Rates move week to week. A good plan shouldn't have to.
>
> This is educational, not financial advice — but those four moves hold up whatever the Fed does next.

### [SAHNE 9 | Ana ve Chris'in masası; kredi teklifi kâğıdı, kalem (Pexels stok) | 6:50–7:55]
> So let's answer the questions.
>
> Why did Ana and Chris's mortgage rate jump if the Fed doesn't set it? Because mortgages follow long-term bond yields, and those rose as markets expected more Fed hikes to fight inflation.
>
> What does a hike do to your card? It nudges a high rate a little higher — about fifteen dollars a year per quarter-point on a 6,000-dollar balance. The level is the real problem.
>
> Do savers win? Only if they move their money, and only by as much as they beat inflation.
>
> And what if the Fed keeps going? Borrowing gets more expensive, and the economy slows — because that's the intention. The Fed raises rates to cool things down. The question is always how much cooling it takes.
>
> Next week on Centworks, just in time for Black Friday: buy now, pay later versus a credit card. Which one actually costs you more?
