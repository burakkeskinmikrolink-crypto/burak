| Zaman | Görsel | Ekran yazısı |

**VO:**
> Paid with Zelle? You're protected?
> For buying from strangers, often not. Zelle moves money from one bank account to another in minutes. If you approved the payment yourself, federal rules generally don't require your bank to return it, even if the item never arrives. Zelle itself says to only send money to people you know and trust. A credit card, by contrast, lets you dispute a purchase that doesn't show up. So before you pay someone you've never met, ask:

| 0–2 sn | **Hareketli:** Kart numarasının rakamları tek tek yanıyor, son rakam altın renginde kilitleniyor (kurgusal numara) | YOUR CARD NUMBER CHECKS ITSELF. |
| 2–12 sn | "Last digit = check digit" | THE LAST DIGIT IS A CHECK |
| 12–22 sn | Ödeme formu: bir rakam yanlış → "Invalid number" | TYPOS CAUGHT INSTANTLY |
| 22–31 sn | İlk rakamlar vurgulu: "network + issuer" | FIRST DIGITS: WHO ISSUED IT |

**VO:**
> Your card number checks itself.
> The last digit on most payment cards is a check digit, calculated from all the others with a simple formula called the Luhn algorithm. Mistype one digit at checkout, and the website can often tell before it ever contacts your bank. It isn't security; anyone can calculate it. It's a typo detector. The first digits matter too: they identify the card network and the bank that issued it. Sixteen digits, and…

**VO:**
> America once had a $10,000 bill.
> There were also five hundred, one thousand, and five thousand dollar notes. They were last printed in 1945. In 1969, the Treasury and the Federal Reserve announced they'd be discontinued because hardly anyone used them. They're still legal tender, but collectors pay far more than face value. Since then, the biggest bill being printed has been the hundred. Hard to believe today, but…

It isn't an accusation. What is illegal is splitting cash into smaller amounts on purpose to avoid the report. That's called structuring, and it can be a crime even if the money is perfectly clean.
