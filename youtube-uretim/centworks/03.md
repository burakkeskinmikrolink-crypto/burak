## Senaryo

### [SAHNE 1 | Yakın plan: bir kuruş masaya düşüp dönüyor (Pixabay: "penny coin spinning") | 0:00–0:13]
> Each penny cost almost four cents to make — so America stopped making them, and now your cash total can get rounded. By the end of this video, you'll know exactly when rounding helps you, and when it doesn't.

### [SAHNE 2 | Fatura, makbuz, hesap makinesi masa üstü (Pexels stok) | 0:13–0:19]
> I spend my evenings reading Federal Reserve research so you don't have to.

### [SAHNE 3 | RECONSTRUCTION: Benzin istasyonu marketi, kasa çekmecesi açılıyor, kuruş bölmesi boş (Pexels stok: "cash register drawer") | 0:19–0:50]
> Picture a convenience-store cashier, late 2025. A customer's total comes to four dollars and ninety-three cents. He hands over a five. The cashier opens the drawer to give back seven cents — a nickel and two pennies.
>
> The penny slot is empty.
>
> This wasn't a one-off. After the U.S. Mint stopped making pennies for circulation, stores in parts of the country reported they couldn't get enough of them. Some began rounding cash totals. One Midwest chain, Kwik Star, rounded cash purchases down — in the customer's favor.
>
> Which is strange, isn't it? Estimates say somewhere between 250 and 300 billion pennies are still out there. So why did stores run out?

### [SAHNE 4 | Gerçek görsel: Philadelphia Darphanesi binası (Wikimedia Commons: "United States Mint Philadelphia"); ABD Darphanesi'nin kamuya açık sent görselleri (usmint.gov – ABD hükümeti eseri) | 0:50–2:05]
> Let's rewind to November 12th, 2025, at the Philadelphia Mint. The Treasurer of the United States struck the final one-cent coins for circulation — ending a run of 232 years. The very last five pennies carry a tiny omega mark, and are expected to be auctioned.
>
> The reason was simple arithmetic. According to the Mint, the cost of making a penny had climbed from 1.42 cents to 3.69 cents over a decade. Every new penny lost the government money.
>
> But stopping production didn't remove pennies from the economy. It changed how they move. Coins don't just circulate on their own — banks order them from the Federal Reserve, and the Fed's network moves them around the country. When production ended, the Fed changed how it handled penny orders and deposits. Shortages followed in some regions, and in January 2026 the Fed reversed course and began accepting penny deposits again.
>
> Meanwhile, Congress caught up. In September 2026, both chambers passed the Common Cents Act. It formally ends penny production and lets businesses round cash transactions to the nearest five cents.
>
> And that brings us to the question everyone actually cares about: when a store rounds, who wins — you, or them?

### [SAHNE 5 | Animasyon: Sayı doğrusu .00 – .10; 1–2 aşağı, 3–4 yukarı, 6–7 aşağı, 8–9 yukarı okları (kendi grafik) | 2:05–3:20]
> Here's how the rounding works. The final cash total — after tax — goes to the nearest nickel. If it ends in one or two cents, it rounds down. Three or four, it rounds up to five. Six or seven, back down to five. Eight or nine, up to the next ten.
>
> So on any single purchase, you can lose up to two cents — or gain up to two cents.
>
> Now, a lot of people assume this is a hidden tax. Economists at the Federal Reserve Bank of Richmond actually ran the numbers. Their estimate? Rounding without the penny would cost American consumers about six million dollars a year in total. Spread across the whole country, that's a rounding error on a rounding error.
>
> Why so small? Because the ups and downs mostly cancel out — and because only cash transactions get rounded.
>
> But there's a twist hiding in that last sentence. And it raises our next question: can a store simply refuse your pennies — or your cash altogether?

### [SAHNE 6 | Gerçek görsel: Federal Reserve binası, Washington (Wikimedia Commons: "Marriner S. Eccles Federal Reserve Board Building"); "LEGAL TENDER" metin grafiği | 3:20–4:30]
> The answer surprises people. U.S. coins and bills are legal tender for debts. But according to the Federal Reserve, there's no federal law that forces a private business to accept cash or coins in the first place. A store can set its own payment policy, unless a state or local law says otherwise.
>
> That's why rounding rules matter. They don't change the price of anything on the shelf. They change what happens at the very last step — when physical money meets a total that coins can no longer match.
>
> And according to CNBC, by August 2026, twenty states had passed their own laws allowing or requiring cash rounding. California's version takes effect in July 2027. So depending on where you live, the rules at the register may already be different from the next state over.
>
> Now, if the penny was too expensive, here's the obvious next question: what about the nickel?

### [SAHNE 7 | ABD Darphanesi kamu görseli: 5 sent (Jefferson nickel); üzerine "13.78¢" etiketi | 4:30–5:40]
> The nickel has the same problem, only bigger. In fiscal year 2024, the Mint reported it cost about 13.78 cents to make one five-cent coin. That's nearly three times its face value.
>
> The Common Cents Act doesn't kill the nickel. Instead, it lets the Mint switch to a cheaper metal mix — if testing shows the new coin wouldn't seriously disrupt vending machines and other coin-operated equipment.
>
> And the Richmond Fed modeled what would happen if the nickel disappeared too, with cash rounded to the nearest dime. Their estimate jumps to about 55.6 million dollars a year in extra cost to consumers — roughly nine times the penny-only figure.
>
> So the penny was the easy one. The nickel is where it starts to cost real money.
>
> But there's one more piece to this puzzle. Why does your card still pay four dollars and ninety-three cents — exactly?

### [SAHNE 8 | Aynı market, bu kez temassız kart ödemesi; ekranda "$4.93" (Pexels stok) | 5:40–6:50]
> Because the rounding only applies to cash. Card payments, phone payments and other electronic transactions keep charging you to the exact cent. The digital penny never died. Only the metal one did.
>
> That creates something new in America: two prices for the same basket. Pay with a card, and you pay four ninety-three. Pay cash, and you pay four ninety-five.
>
> Other countries have been here. Canada stopped distributing pennies in 2013, and rounded cash totals to the nearest five cents while card payments stayed exact. Life carried on.
>
> And the stakes for shoppers are tiny — a couple of cents either way. The bigger shift is cultural. For two centuries, a price tag in America was a promise of the exact amount you'd hand over. Now, for cash, it's a close estimate.

### [SAHNE 9 | Kavanoz dolusu bozuk para (Pixabay: "coin jar"); bankada bozuk para sayma makinesi | 6:50–7:55]
> So let's answer our questions.
>
> Why did stores run out when billions of pennies exist? Because most of those pennies are sitting in jars and drawers, not moving through the system — and the system that moved them changed.
>
> Who wins from rounding? On average, nobody by much. You might gain two cents on one purchase and lose two on the next. The Fed's estimate for the whole country is about six million dollars a year.
>
> Can a store refuse cash? At the federal level, yes — unless your state or city says otherwise.
>
> And here's the practical move. If you've got a jar of pennies at home, it's still real money. Roll them, or take them to a bank or a coin counter, and turn them into something the system can use. Those coins aren't worthless. They just stopped moving.
>
> Next time on Centworks: who's really paying for your credit card rewards. The answer might be the person in line behind you.
