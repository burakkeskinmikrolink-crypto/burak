> Zero percent beats twenty-one percent — yet nearly half of buy-now-pay-later users say they've paid late. Here's which is really cheaper this Black Friday weekend.

### [SAHNE 2 | Fiş, kart, hesap makinesi (Pexels) | 0:12–0:16]
> This channel is made by an engineer, not a banker.

### [SAHNE 3 | RECONSTRUCTION: Gece, kanepe, telefon ekranında 600 dolarlık TV sepeti; iki ödeme butonu (kendi grafik), yüz yok | Kesme: 3–4 sn | 0:16–0:45]
> Last night, Black Friday, Jordan had a 600-dollar TV in the cart. Two buttons. "Pay in four, zero interest." Or the credit card, at the kind of rate Federal Reserve data puts near twenty-one percent.
>
> Easy, right? Jordan taps "pay in four" — then does it again for a jacket and a console.
>
> If it's interest-free, how do these companies make money?
>
> And with Cyber Monday coming, Jordan's about to find out.

*(Ekranda: "Illustrative example")*

### [SAHNE 4 | Animasyon: Müşteri → BNPL şirketi → mağaza; mağazadan BNPL'e giden ücret oku (kendi grafik). Puan tablosu: "Round 1: Who pays?" | 0:45–2:00]
> Here's the hidden system. In the classic "pay in four" model, the store pays. The buy-now-pay-later company pays the merchant up front, minus a fee, and collects the money from you over six weeks.
>
> Why would a store accept that? Because people who see "four easy payments" tend to buy more, and more often. It's the same logic as the credit card swipe fees we covered in our rewards video. Somebody always pays for the convenience.
>
> The second income stream is you, if things go wrong: late fees, on some products. And on longer plans — six, twelve, twenty-four months — many providers do charge interest. One major provider advertises rates from zero up to thirty-six percent APR, depending on the loan.
>
> That's why the checkout button and the fine print can look so different. The button says "zero percent." The terms page explains which plan you actually picked, what happens if a payment fails, and whether a longer plan was offered instead.
>
> So "buy now, pay later" isn't one product. It's a family of products, and only some of them are free.
>
> Round one goes to nobody: both systems are paid for, partly, through store prices.
>
> But here's a puzzle. The official data says these loans mostly go fine. So why do so many users say they've paid late?

### [SAHNE 5 | İki çubuk: "4.1% of loans hit with a late fee (CFPB, 2023 data)" ve "47% say they paid late (LendingTree survey, 2026)". Puan tablosu: "Round 2: Late payments" | 2:00–3:15]
> Two numbers, measuring two different things.
>
> In December 2025, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau published a report on pay-in-four loans from six big providers, using 2023 data. That year, 53.6 million Americans took out at least one of these loans. The share of loans hit with a late fee was 4.1 percent — down from 5.2 percent the year before.
>
> But a 2026 survey by LendingTree found that 47 percent of BNPL users said they'd paid late at least once in the past year. That's an online survey, not loan records — but it measures something the official data can't: people, not loans.
>
> How can both be true? Take out six loans and pay one late, and most of your loans were on time. But you, as a person, paid late. And the CFPB report notes average usage of about six loans per user, per lender — before counting people using several apps at once.
>
> That's called loan stacking. Round two goes to the credit card, because one statement is easier to track than six schedules. And it raises another question: if you stack these loans, does anyone see it?

### [SAHNE 6 | Animasyon: Kredi raporu; kart satırları net, BNPL satırları silik (kendi grafik). Puan tablosu: "Round 3: Protection" | 3:15–4:30]
> Often, not really. Historically, most pay-in-four loans haven't shown up on credit reports the way credit cards do. FICO announced new scores in 2025 designed to include buy-now-pay-later data, but it's still unclear how widely lenders use them.
>
> That cuts both ways. Paying on time may not build your credit. Paying late, on the other hand, can still lead to fees, collections, or a blocked account with that provider. And a lender — or you — may not see the full picture of what you owe across apps.
>
> There's one more difference most shoppers never think about. What happens if the TV never arrives?
>
> With a credit card, federal law — the Fair Credit Billing Act — gives you the right to dispute charges for goods you didn't receive. In 2024, the CFPB said buy-now-pay-later lenders should offer similar protections. In 2025, it withdrew that interpretation. Many providers do have their own dispute processes, but those depend on each company's terms.
>
> So before counting on that protection for a big purchase, read what the app actually promises. Round three goes to the credit card.

### [SAHNE 7 | Karşılaştırma tablosu: 4 senaryo, maliyet sütunu. Puan tablosu: "Round 4: The $600 TV" | 4:30–5:45]
> Now the real test: Jordan's 600-dollar TV, four ways.
>
> Scenario one: pay in four, all on time. Total cost: 600 dollars. Hard to beat.
>
> Scenario two: credit card, paid in full when the statement arrives. Thanks to the grace period, no interest. Also 600 dollars — and with a rewards card, maybe a few dollars back.
>
> Scenario three: credit card, paid off at a hundred dollars a month at twenty-one percent. That takes seven months and costs roughly forty dollars in interest.
>
> Scenario four: pay in four, stacked with other plans, and one payment bounces. Now there may be a late fee, depending on the provider, an overdraft fee from the bank if the payment hits an empty account, and four due dates nobody planned for.
>
> Round four is a tie. Used perfectly, both cost exactly 600 dollars. Used carelessly, both cost more.

*(Ekranda: "Illustrative example — fees vary by provider and bank")*

### [SAHNE 8 | Tek cümle büyük yazıyla: "Can I pay the full price today?" | 5:45–6:50]
> So the deciding factor isn't the product. It's this question: could you pay the full price today?
>
> If you can, a credit card paid off every month is usually the strongest choice: zero interest, purchase protection, and maybe rewards.
>
> If you need a few weeks — say, until your next paycheck — a single pay-in-four plan, paid on time from a funded account, really can cost nothing. One plan. Not four.
>
> And if the answer is no, neither button makes it affordable. They just change how the cost shows up.
>
> Before Cyber Monday, a few habits help with both. Put every due date in one calendar. Turn on autopay from an account that will actually have the money. Check whether a longer plan charges interest. And before tapping, ask: "Would I buy this if I had to pay all of it right now?"

### [SAHNE 9 | Puan tablosu son hâli: Card 2 – BNPL 0 – Tie 2; Jordan'ın telefonundan iki ürün sepetten çıkıyor | 6:50–7:50]
> Final score: the credit card wins on tracking and protection. The two tie on cost — if, and only if, they're used with discipline. And buy now, pay later never wins a round outright.
>
> That doesn't make it bad. It makes it a tool with a narrow sweet spot: one plan, a short gap, paid on time.
>
> Jordan kept the TV, and deleted the jacket and the console. Not because the apps are evil, but because six weeks comes around faster than you think, and Cyber Monday is only two days away.
>
> This is educational, not financial advice. The cheapest button is still the one you can pay off before the next bill arrives.
