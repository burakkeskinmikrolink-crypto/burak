## Neden bu konu (not)
Black Friday ve Cyber Monday, BNPL kullanımının zirve yaptığı dönem. Elde iki farklı tablo var. CFPB'nin Aralık 2025 raporunda (2023 verisi) gecikme ücreti ve zarar oranları düşüyor. LendingTree'nin 2026 anketinde ise kullanıcıların %47'si son bir yılda geç ödediğini söylüyor. Ayrıca CFPB, BNPL'e kredi kartı benzeri itiraz hakkı tanıyan 2024 yorum kuralını 2025'te geri çekti. Kanalın önceki ödül kartı videosuyla (04) doğal bir bağ var.

> ⚠️ **Yayın öncesi kontrol:** BNPL sağlayıcılarının gecikme ücreti politikaları değişebiliyor. Metin bilinçli olarak tek tek şirket ücreti vermiyor ("check the terms" diyor). Affirm'in "0–36% APR" ifadesini kendi sitesinden kontrol et. FICO BNPL skorunun kredi verenlerce ne kadar kullanıldığı belirsiz, metin de belirsiz bırakıyor.

### [SAHNE 2 | Masa üstü: CFPB raporu çıktısı, hesap makinesi (Pexels stok) | 0:14–0:19]
> I spend my evenings reading Federal Reserve research so you don't have to.

### [SAHNE 3 | RECONSTRUCTION: Gece, kanepe, telefon ekranında 600 dolarlık TV sepeti; iki ödeme butonu yan yana (kendi grafik) | 0:19–0:52]
> It's Black Friday night. Jordan has a 600-dollar TV in the cart. At checkout, two buttons.
>
> Button one: "Pay in four. Zero interest." A hundred and fifty today, then a hundred and fifty every two weeks.
>
> Button two: the credit card. Twenty-one percent interest, according to the average rate in Federal Reserve data.
>
> It looks like the easiest decision in the world. Zero percent beats twenty-one percent. Every time. Right?
>
> Jordan taps "pay in four." And then, in the same week, does the same thing for a jacket, a game console and a pair of boots — with two different apps.
>
> So here's our first question: if buy now, pay later is interest-free, how do the companies behind it make money?

*(Ekranda: "Illustrative example")*

### [SAHNE 4 | Animasyon: Müşteri → BNPL şirketi → mağaza; mağazadan BNPL'e giden ücret oku (kendi grafik) | 0:52–2:05]
> Here's the hidden system. In the classic "pay in four" model, the store pays. The buy-now-pay-later company pays the merchant up front, minus a fee, and collects the money from you over six weeks.
>
> Why would a store accept that? Because people who see "four easy payments" tend to buy more, and buy more often. Sound familiar? It's the same logic as the credit card swipe fees we covered in our rewards video. Somebody always pays for the convenience.
>
> The second income stream is you, if things go wrong: late fees, on some products. And on longer plans — six, twelve, twenty-four months — many providers do charge interest. One major provider advertises rates from zero up to thirty-six percent APR, depending on the loan.
>
> So "buy now, pay later" isn't one product. It's a family of products, and only some of them are free.
>
> Now, the official data says these loans mostly go fine. So why do so many users say they've paid late?

### [SAHNE 5 | Gerçek görsel: CFPB raporu kapağı yerine metin grafiği; iki çubuk: "4.1% assessed a late fee (CFPB, 2023 data)" ve "47% say they paid late (LendingTree survey, 2026)" | 2:05–3:15]
> Two numbers, measuring two different things.
>
> In December 2025, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau published a report on pay-in-four loans from six big providers, using 2023 data. That year, 53.6 million Americans took out at least one of these loans. The share of loans hit with a late fee was 4.1 percent — down from 5.2 percent the year before.
>
> But a 2026 survey by LendingTree found that 47 percent of BNPL users said they'd paid late at least once in the past year. That's an online survey, not loan records — but it measures something the official data can't: people, not loans.
>
> Here's how both can be true. If you take out six loans and pay one late, most of your loans were on time. But you, as a person, paid late. And the CFPB report notes that average usage was about six loans per user, per lender — before counting people using several apps at once.
>
> That's called loan stacking. And it raises our next question: if you stack these loans, does anyone actually see it?

### [SAHNE 6 | Animasyon: Kredi raporu; kredi kartı satırları görünüyor, BNPL satırları silik/gölgede (kendi grafik) | 3:15–4:30]
> Often, not really. Historically, most pay-in-four loans haven't shown up on credit reports the way credit cards do. FICO announced new scores in 2025 designed to include buy-now-pay-later data, but it's still unclear how widely lenders use them.
>
> That cuts both ways. Paying on time may not build your credit. And a lender — or you — may not see the full picture of what you owe across apps.
>
> Credit cards are the opposite: everything on one statement, one balance, one due date, visible to every lender who checks.
>
> There's one more difference most shoppers never think about. What happens when the TV never arrives?
>
> With a credit card, federal law — the Fair Credit Billing Act — gives you the right to dispute charges for goods you didn't receive. In 2024, the CFPB said buy-now-pay-later lenders should offer similar protections. In 2025, it withdrew that interpretation. Many providers do have their own dispute processes — but those depend on each company's terms. So before you count on that protection for a big purchase, read what the app actually promises — not what you assume it promises.

### [SAHNE 7 | Karşılaştırma tablosu grafiği: 4 senaryo, maliyet sütunu | 4:30–5:40]
> So let's run Jordan's 600-dollar TV through four scenarios.
>
> Scenario one: pay in four, all on time. Total cost: 600 dollars. Hard to beat.
>
> Scenario two: credit card, paid in full when the statement arrives. Thanks to the grace period, no interest. Total: 600 dollars — and if it's a rewards card, you might get a few dollars back.
>
> Scenario three: credit card, paid off at a hundred dollars a month at twenty-one percent. That takes seven months and costs roughly forty dollars in interest.
>
> Scenario four: pay in four — but stacked with three other plans, and one payment bounces. Now you may face a late fee, depending on the provider, an overdraft fee from your bank if the payment hits an empty account, and the stress of four due dates you didn't plan for.
>
> So which is cheaper? It depends on one thing. And it's not the product.

*(Ekranda: "Illustrative example — fees vary by provider and bank")*

### [SAHNE 8 | Sakin grafik: tek cümle büyük yazıyla, "Can I pay the full price today?" | 5:40–6:50]
> Here's the rule: if you can't afford it in full today, neither option makes it affordable. They just change how the cost shows up.
>
> If you can pay in full, a credit card paid off every month is usually the strongest choice: zero interest, purchase protection, and maybe rewards.
>
> If you need a few weeks — say, until your next paycheck — a single pay-in-four plan, paid on time from a funded account, really can cost nothing. One plan. Not four.
>
> And a few habits that help with both. Put every due date in one calendar. Turn on autopay from an account that will actually have the money. Read whether a longer plan charges interest. And before tapping the button, ask: "Would I buy this if I had to pay all of it right now?"
>
> This is educational, not financial advice. But that question has saved more money than any app.

### [SAHNE 9 | Jordan'ın telefonu; sepetten iki ürün çıkarılıyor, tek ödeme planı kalıyor (kendi grafik) | 6:50–7:55]
> So let's answer our questions.
>
> How do interest-free loans make money? Mostly from merchants, who pay to offer them — and, for some products, from late fees and interest on longer plans.
>
> Why do so many users pay late when the official data looks healthy? Because the data counts loans, the surveys count people, and one person can juggle many loans.
>
> Does anyone see the stack? Often not — not lenders, and sometimes not even you.
>
> And which is cheaper, A or B? Pay in four wins when it's one plan, paid on time. A credit card wins when it's paid in full. Both lose when they're used to buy something you can't afford yet.
>
> Jordan kept the TV, and deleted the jacket and the boots. Not because the apps are bad, but because six weeks comes around faster than you think.
