## Neden bu konu (not)
Küba'nın ulusal şebekesi 2026'da defalarca tamamen çöktü. UPI 18 Eylül'deki çöküşü yılın **yedincisi** olarak sayıyor. Kanalın İspanya videosundan farklı bir açısı var: o video sağlam bir şebekenin tek seferlik çöküşünü anlatıyordu, bu video bakımsızlık ve yakıt kıtlığının yarattığı **kronik** çöküşü anlatıyor. Asıl ders kısmı da yeni: **"black start"**, yani karanlıkta kalmış bir şebekenin nasıl yeniden başlatıldığı. İngiltere kitlesi için NESO'nun "5 günde %100 geri getirme" standardı bağlantısı var.

> ⚠️ **Siyasi hassasiyet:** Küba hükümeti çöküşlerin sebebi olarak ABD yaptırımlarını ve yakıt ablukasını gösteriyor. Bağımsız analistler ise yıllardır yapılmayan yatırımı ve bakımsızlığı da sayıyor (UPI). Metin iki tarafı da veriyor ve kendisi taraf tutmuyor. Bir kısım kaynak (CiberCuba, WSWS) taraflı yayın organı olarak biliniyor. Onlardan gelen ayrıntılar "according to reports" diye işaretlendi.

> ⚠️ **Sayım farkı:** Kaynaklar 2026'daki çöküş sayısında anlaşamıyor. CNN 2 Ağustos'takini "altıncı", UPI 18 Eylül'dekini "yedinci" diyor. Bazıları kısmi çöküşleri de sayıyor. Metin "seven times this year, by one count" diyor. Yayına kadar yeni çöküş olursa güncelle.

## Senaryo

### [SAHNE 1 | NASA "Black Marble" gece uydu görüntüsü: Karayipler, Küba ışıklı → animasyonla kararıyor (NASA kamu malı) | 0:00–0:14]
> Cuba's whole power grid has collapsed seven times this year, by one count — and each time, the country is restarted from darkness. By the end, you'll know how you restart a dead grid — and why Cuba's keeps dying.

### [SAHNE 2 | NOC ekran duvarı, baz istasyonlarının "on battery" durumları (kendi grafik) | 0:14–0:20]
> I work in a mobile network operations centre, where a power cut is a countdown.

### [SAHNE 3 | RECONSTRUCTION: Havana silueti alacakaranlıkta (Wikimedia Commons: "Havana skyline at dusk"); mutfakta duran buzdolabı, boş su musluğu (Pexels stok) | 0:20–0:52]
> It's a Friday afternoon in September, in Havana. Fans slow down and stop. Fridges go quiet. Somewhere, an electric water pump that fills a rooftop tank simply… stops pushing.
>
> It isn't a local fault. Cuba's state grid operator, the Unión Eléctrica, confirms the national system has collapsed. The entire island.
>
> By nightfall, according to reports, only around five percent of Havana's customers have power again — mostly around hospitals.
>
> For many Cubans, this isn't shocking any more. It's familiar. Earlier in the summer, the grid had collapsed three times in nine days.
>
> So here's our first question: how can a whole country's electricity fail at once — and then fail again, and again?

### [SAHNE 4 | Animasyon: Terazi; bir kefede "generation", diğerinde "demand"; ortada 60 Hz ibresi. Büyük bir santral düşünce ibre sert düşüyor ve diğer santraller tek tek kopuyor (kendi grafik) | 0:52–2:05]
> To understand that, you need to see a grid the way engineers do: not as wires, but as a balance.
>
> At every instant, the power being generated has to match the power being used. Cuba's grid runs at sixty hertz — sixty cycles a second — and that frequency is the heartbeat that tells you whether the balance is holding. If a big power station suddenly drops out, demand outweighs supply and the frequency falls.
>
> Every other power station has automatic protection. If the frequency falls too far, they disconnect to protect themselves. But each one that leaves makes the imbalance worse. That's a cascade. In seconds, a grid can go from one fault to zero.
>
> Now, Cuba's grid has a particular weakness. It leans heavily on a small number of large, ageing oil-fired power stations. The biggest, the Antonio Guiteras plant in Matanzas, opened in 1988. When one machine supplies a big slice of a small grid, its failure can pull everything else down with it. In October 2024, a failure at Guiteras was followed by a nationwide blackout that lasted days.
>
> But a collapse is only half the story. Once it's all dark, how do you switch a country back on?

### [SAHNE 5 | Gerçek görsel: termik santral kazan dairesi ve türbin (Wikimedia Commons: "Category:Steam turbines"); animasyon: haritada küçük ışık adaları beliriyor, sonra birleşiyor | 2:05–3:15]
> This is the hidden system almost nobody knows exists. It's called black start.
>
> Here's the problem. A big thermal power station needs electricity to start: for its pumps, its fans, its control systems. If the whole grid is dead, there's nothing to start it with.
>
> So engineers begin small. A diesel generator, or a hydro plant, brings one station's auxiliaries to life. That station energises a small island of the network — Cuban authorities call these microsystems — often built around hospitals and water supplies. Then the islands are carefully synchronised, matched to the exact same frequency and phase, and joined together.
>
> It's slow and delicate. Any wobble can collapse the islands too. And that's exactly what Cubans have watched happen. In early August, according to UPI, the grid collapsed again just twenty-one hours after power had been restored. The Unión Eléctrica said storms then disrupted the restoration, affecting the generating units that were back online.
>
> Which raises the question underneath all of this. Why does the system keep starting from such a fragile place?

### [SAHNE 6 | Gerçek görsel: petrol tankeri (NOAA/ABD kamu malı liman fotoğrafı veya Pixabay); Guiteras yerine genel termik santral bacası (Wikimedia Commons) | 3:15–4:30]
> The answer has two parts — and depending on who you ask, one matters more than the other.
>
> Part one is fuel. Most of Cuba's electricity comes from burning oil. In early 2026, shipments from Venezuela, long a key supplier, stopped. The Cuban government blames a US fuel blockade and sanctions for the shortages.
>
> Part two is the machinery itself. International and independent analysts, as UPI notes, point to years of underinvestment and poor economic management. According to reports in Cuban independent media, the Guiteras plant hasn't had a major overhaul since 2010, and its director has said it needs months offline for repairs that the country can't currently afford to give it.
>
> Put those together and you get a grid with almost no spare capacity. Engineers call that margin. When there's no margin, any single failure — a boiler leak, a transmission line, a storm — becomes a national event.
>
> So what does living without that margin actually look like?

### [SAHNE 7 | Gerçek görsel: Havana'da su tankları olan çatılar (Wikimedia Commons); baz istasyonu kulesi (Wikimedia Commons: "Category:Cell towers") | 4:30–5:40]
> It starts with water. In many buildings, water only reaches your tap because an electric pump puts it there. The US Embassy in Havana warned that the outages were affecting water supply, lighting, refrigeration and communications.
>
> In late October, the news agency AFP described Cubans digging wells in search of water — and reported that one of Havana's main water sources, the Albear Aqueduct, had just been fitted with two large diesel backup generators.
>
> And then there's my world: mobile networks. Phone masts normally carry batteries, and some have generators. Batteries buy hours, not days. When a blackout outlasts them, the mast goes quiet — and so does the phone in your hand, exactly when you need it.
>
> Which brings us to the question British and American viewers are probably asking. Could this happen somewhere wealthier?

### [SAHNE 8 | Gerçek görsel: İngiltere yüksek gerilim hatları (Wikimedia Commons: "Category:Electricity pylons in England"); "5 days / 100%" metin grafiği | 5:40–6:50]
> A total blackout, yes. We covered one on this channel: Spain, in April 2025. The cause was different, but the physics of the collapse was similar. What was very different was the recovery. Spain had fuel, spare capacity, connections to its neighbours, and rehearsed restart plans. Most of the country had power back within about a day.
>
> Cuba is an island grid. It can't borrow power from next door.
>
> In Britain, black start is planned in advance. The government has set an Electricity System Restoration Standard. By the end of 2026, the system operator, NESO, must be able to restore sixty percent of demand in every region within twenty-four hours, and all of it nationally within five days.
>
> That's the real lesson. A grid doesn't just need power stations. It needs a plan for the day they all stop — and the fuel, the spare capacity and the maintenance to make that plan work.

### [SAHNE 9 | NOC ekranı; batarya göstergeleri tek tek sıfıra iniyor, sonra bir ada yeşile dönüyor | 6:50–7:55]
> So, our questions. How can a whole country fail at once? Because a grid is a balance, and when a big plant drops out of a small, stretched system, protection systems pull the rest offline in seconds.
>
> How do you switch a country back on? Island by island, from a black start — slowly, carefully, and hoping nothing wobbles.
>
> And why does Cuba's keep dying? Because it's running with almost no margin: not enough fuel, and ageing machines that can't be taken offline long enough to fix them. The Cuban government and its critics disagree about which matters more. Both are part of the story.
>
> In my operations centre, we plan for the moment the power goes. But every plan quietly assumes something: that the power will come back. In Cuba this year, millions of people have learnt what happens when that assumption breaks.
