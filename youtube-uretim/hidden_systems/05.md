## Neden bu konu (not)
Kanalın "şirket çöküşleri" ayağı için en güçlü güncel hikâye bu. ABD'li otomobil parçası üreticisi First Brands Eylül 2025'te iflas başvurusu yaptı. Ağustos 2026'da yargıç planı reddetti ve dosya Chapter 7 tasfiyesine çevrildi. Ceza davası 2027'de bekleniyor. Görünmeyen sistem burada **fatura finansmanı (factoring)** ve bilanço dışı borç. İngiltere'deki kitle için Greensill (2021) bağlantısı ve UBS/Jefferies gibi bilinen kurumların zararları var.

> ⚖️ **Hukuki dil kuralı:** Kurucu ve kardeşi suçlamaları reddetti ve masumiyet karinesi geçerli. Metinde suçlamalar her zaman "prosecutors allege" ya da "according to the indictment" diye veriliyor. Sadece eski CFO'nun suçu kabul ettiği kesin olgu olarak geçiyor. Gerçek kişi fotoğrafı **kullanma**.

> ⚠️ **Yayın öncesi kontrol:** Ceza davasının tarihi kaynaklarda farklı (önce 13 Temmuz 2026 deniyordu, Bloomberg 8 Ekim 2026'da Şubat diyor). Metinde "a criminal trial is expected next year" deniyor. Yayından önce dava takvimine bak.

> A company owing over nine billion dollars had just twelve million in the bank when it collapsed. By the end of this video, you'll know how one invoice can be sold twice — and why nobody noticed.

### [SAHNE 2 | NOC ekran duvarı, yeşil ışıklar (Pexels: "server monitoring") | 0:15–0:20]
> I work in a mobile network operations centre, where the dangerous failures are the silent ones.

### [SAHNE 3 | RECONSTRUCTION: Gece ofisi, tek monitörde bir tablo, sütunlardan biri boş kalıyor (Pexels stok: "office night spreadsheet") | 0:20–0:55]
> A finance team at an investment fund is watching a column of numbers it has watched for years. Every few days, money arrives — payments from car-parts retailers, flowing through a supplier, into the fund.
>
> And then, in the middle of September 2025, the money stops.
>
> Not a delay. Not a bank holiday. According to the investment bank Jefferies, its affiliated fund was told that First Brands — a giant American maker of wiper blades, filters and brake parts — had stopped passing on customer payments.
>
> Thirteen days later, First Brands filed for bankruptcy.
>
> So here's our first question: how does a company that sells parts in shops across America run out of money — and nobody on the outside notices until the very end?

### [SAHNE 4 | Gerçek görsel: Otomobil parçası rafları, silecek, yağ filtresi (Pexels/Pixabay: "auto parts store shelf") | 0:55–2:05]
> To understand this, we need the hidden system underneath almost every business: the invoice.
>
> When First Brands ships a box of brake parts to a retailer, the retailer doesn't pay that day. It might pay in sixty or ninety days. That piece of paper — "you owe us" — is an asset. And assets can be sold.
>
> That's called factoring. A specialist buys the invoice today, at a small discount. The supplier gets cash now. The buyer of the invoice collects later, and keeps the difference.
>
> To make that concrete — and these are illustrative numbers, not First Brands' — imagine a retailer owes a hundred thousand dollars in ninety days. A finance firm might pay ninety-seven thousand today. Three months later, the retailer pays the full hundred, and the finance firm keeps three thousand for its trouble. Repeat that thousands of times a month, and you've built a machine that turns future payments into cash right now.
>
> It's old, normal and perfectly legal. Small firms use it every day. But there's a detail that matters enormously. In many arrangements, the retailer never learns that the invoice was sold. It keeps paying the supplier as usual — and the supplier is trusted to forward the money.
>
> Think about that. The money owed to the lender travels through the hands of the company that owes it.
>
> Which leads to our second question: if an invoice is just a promise on paper, what stops someone selling the same promise twice?

### [SAHNE 5 | Animasyon: tek bir fatura üç kopyaya bölünüyor, üç farklı finansçıya gidiyor (kendi motion graphic) | 2:05–3:15]
> On the twenty-eighth of September 2025, First Brands filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy in Texas. The filings described more than nine billion dollars in liabilities — and about twelve million dollars of cash in its corporate bank accounts.
>
> Lenders started comparing notes. And investigators began asking whether the same receivables had been financed more than once.
>
> In January 2026, federal prosecutors in New York unsealed an indictment against the company's founder, Patrick James, and his brother. They allege fake or inflated invoices, collateral pledged to more than one lender — in some cases, prosecutors say, to three — false financial statements, and hidden off-balance-sheet financing. Both brothers pleaded not guilty.
>
> In March, the company's former finance chief pleaded guilty to fraud charges, and is expected to testify.
>
> But there's a deeper question here — one that should worry anyone who's ever read a company's accounts. How could billions in debt be invisible?

### [SAHNE 6 | Animasyon: bilanço; "Debt" sütunu küçük görünüyor, alttan buz dağı gibi büyük bir gölge çıkıyor | 3:15–4:30]
> Here's the trick of the system — not of the alleged fraud, of the system itself.
>
> When a company borrows from a bank, that's debt. It sits on the balance sheet where everyone can see it. But when a company sells its invoices, that can be treated as a sale — not a loan. Cash comes in, but the debt line doesn't grow.
>
> Done honestly, that's fine. But it means a company can be financed far more heavily than its accounts suggest. The company's own bankruptcy estate has since alleged that the founder used fake invoices to run up at least 2.3 billion dollars in factoring liabilities, and separate special-purpose vehicles to take on at least 2.3 billion more. He disputes that.
>
> In my world, we'd call this a monitoring gap. Every lender saw its own slice. Nobody saw the whole network. In a phone network, each piece of equipment can report that it's healthy while the service as a whole is broken — which is exactly why we watch the end-to-end picture, not just the boxes.
>
> So who was left holding the losses?

### [SAHNE 7 | Gerçek görsel: Londra City ve Zürih silueti (Wikimedia Commons, telifsiz lisanslı şehir fotoğrafları) → isimsiz fon ikonları | 4:30–5:45]
> The list reads like a who's who of finance. The Jefferies-affiliated fund Point Bonita had around 715 million dollars tied to First Brands receivables — roughly a quarter of its trade-finance book. Court filings showed funds linked to UBS with more than 500 million dollars of exposure. A specialist trade-finance firm, Raistone, appeared among the creditors.
>
> Jefferies says it was a victim of fraud. Investors in its fund have sued, alleging they were misled about who controlled the cash.
>
> And in August 2026, the bankruptcy judge rejected the company's rescue plan, saying it was no longer a viable business. The case was converted to Chapter 7 — liquidation. The company would be sold off in pieces.
>
> If this sounds familiar to British viewers, it should. Which brings us to the last question: hasn't the financial world seen this movie before?

### [SAHNE 8 | Gerçek görsel: Westminster / Parlamento binası (Wikimedia Commons) → "Lessons from Greensill Capital" rapor kapağı yerine metin grafiği | 5:45–6:55]
> In March 2021, Greensill Capital — a London-based supply-chain finance firm — collapsed. Credit Suisse had to freeze around ten billion dollars of investment funds linked to it. A House of Commons committee later published a report titled, simply, "Lessons from Greensill Capital".
>
> Different company, different country, different details. But the same hidden system: short-term finance built on invoices and future payments, sold on to investors who mostly saw the paperwork, not the trucks.
>
> The lesson both stories share is uncomfortable. Trade finance is meant to be one of the safest kinds of lending, because it's backed by real goods. But it's only as safe as the paperwork is true — and as the system's ability to notice when the same paper turns up twice.

### [SAHNE 9 | NOC ekranı, tek bir yeşil ışık kırmızıya dönüyor; ardından boş otomobil parçası rafı | 6:55–8:00]
> So, how does a company run out of money without anyone noticing? Because each lender watched only its own slice — and the money it was owed passed through the very company that owed it.
>
> How can an invoice be sold twice? Prosecutors allege it was. A jury hasn't ruled yet; a criminal trial is expected next year.
>
> And why didn't anyone see the debt? Because the system allowed much of it to look like something else.
>
> In my operations centre, the scariest alarm is the one that never fires — a link that keeps reporting "all fine" while nothing is moving through it. First Brands was the financial version: green lights everywhere, until the money stopped.
>
> The parts are still on the shelves. The invoices behind them are now evidence.

## Shorts (3 adet, farklı formatlar)

### Short 1: "60 saniyede sistem" anlatımı (40 sn)
- **Görsel:** Elle çizilmiş tarzda animasyon: Fabrika → Mağaza → Fatura → Finansçı.
- **VO:** Here's a hidden system most people never think about. A factory ships parts to a shop. The shop pays in ninety days. The factory doesn't want to wait, so it sells that invoice to a lender for cash today. Normal. Legal. Everywhere. But often, the shop keeps paying the factory, and the factory forwards the money. Now imagine the factory stops forwarding it. That's what investors say happened at First Brands, weeks before a nine-billion-dollar bankruptcy. Full story on Hidden Systems.

### Short 2: "Rakam karşılaştırma" (30 sn)
- **Ekran:** Solda "$9,000,000,000+" (kırmızı), sağda "$12,000,000" (gri).

Prosecutors allege fake invoices and collateral pledged to more than one lender. The defendants pleaded not guilty. The former finance chief pleaded guilty. The company is now being liquidated.

### Short 3: "İngiltere bağlantısı" (35 sn)
- **Görsel:** Londra City silueti (Wikimedia) → Teksas'ta bir mahkeme binası çizimi.
- **VO:** British viewers, this will feel familiar. In 2021, Greensill Capital collapsed in London, and Credit Suisse froze around ten billion dollars of funds tied to it. In 2025, a US auto-parts company called First Brands collapsed, leaving funds linked to Jefferies and UBS exposed. Different details, same hidden system: finance built on invoices that investors rarely see behind. I work in network operations. We call that a monitoring gap.
