## Senaryo

### [SAHNE 1 | Siyah ekran → yakın plan kart terminali, ekranda "DECLINED" (Pexels stok: "card terminal") | 0:00–0:15]
> One building in London lost power — and for most of a working day, Britons couldn't reach their own money. By the end of this video, you'll know the machine that was meant to stop that — and why it didn't.

### [SAHNE 2 | Gece çekimi telekom kontrol odası, ekran duvarı (Pexels/Pixabay: "network operations center") | 0:15–0:20]
> I work in a mobile network operations centre, and power failure is what we rehearse most.

### [SAHNE 3 | RECONSTRUCTION: Sabah trafiği, otobüs durağı, temassız ödeme (Pexels stok); ardından loş sunucu koridoru (Wikimedia Commons: "Category:Server rooms") | 0:20–0:55]
> It's just after seven in the morning, British Summer Time. Commuters are tapping phones at barriers. Somewhere, a Revolut customer taps a card at a till. Nothing. Taps again. Declined.
>
> A few miles away, inside a data centre, the incoming mains supply falters. And here's the thing — that should be a non-event. Buildings like this are designed on the assumption that the grid will fail. Banks of batteries take the load instantly. Then, within moments, diesel generators the size of shipping containers are supposed to start, synchronise, and carry the entire building.
>
> That morning, the batteries took the load. The generators stayed silent.
>
> So hold onto this question, because we'll come back to it: how does a building designed around the certainty of power cuts get beaten by… a power cut?

### [SAHNE 4 | Animasyon: Birleşik Krallık haritası → Londra'da tek nokta → üç kutu "Zone A / B / C" (kendi motion graphic'in); Google Cloud dokümanından bölge-zon şeması yeniden çizilmiş | 0:55–2:05]
> First, we need to strip away a word. "The cloud." It sounds weightless. It isn't. It's buildings — full of servers, cooling plant, and very large batteries.
>
> Google sells computing in what it calls regions. Its London region has the code name europe-west2. Each region is split into zones — separate deployment areas, so that if one fails, the others carry on. Google's own documentation tells customers that if they want to stay up, they should spread across more than one zone.
>
> At about eight minutes past seven, one of London's three zones — europe-west2-b — lost power.
>
> Now let's follow the electricity. Power arrives from the grid. If it drops, an uninterruptible power supply — a UPS — bridges the gap with batteries. Those batteries are a bridge, not a destination: they're sized to last minutes, not hours. Their only job is to keep everything alive until the generators take over.
>
> And the generators don't decide for themselves. They're started by automation — industrial controllers called PLCs, programmable logic controllers. Small grey boxes. They watch the supply, and when it fails, they give the order: start, sync, transfer.
>
> But that raises a bigger question than one zone. The whole point of three zones is that you can lose one. So why did banking apps, court hearings and parcel firms all fall over together?

### [SAHNE 5 | RECONSTRUCTION: Nöbetçi mühendis siluetiyle koridor, kırmızı alarm ışığı (Pexels stok); UPS akü dizileri (Wikimedia Commons: "Category:Uninterruptible power supplies") | 2:05–3:15]
> Back inside the zone, the clock is running. The batteries are draining with every second. The generators still haven't started.
>
> According to Google's preliminary report, as summarised by independent monitoring firms, when the UPS batteries ran down, the servers, network equipment and storage in that zone lost power.
>
> Outside, the effects spread. Revolut reported problems with card payments, transfers, cash withdrawals and logging in. Other banks and businesses reported trouble too. HM Courts and Tribunals Service said the video platform used for remote hearings was disrupted. Delivery companies, including Royal Mail and DPD, were reported among those affected. Google's status page eventually listed dozens of its own cloud products as impacted.
>
> In my world, this is the moment the screens turn red — not one alarm, but hundreds, as every system that depended on that building reports that it can't reach something.
>
> And here's the part that should worry regulators more than any generator: why were so many different British institutions relying on the same piece of infrastructure in the first place?

### [SAHNE 6 | Gerçek görsel: dizel jeneratör seti (Wikimedia Commons: "Category:Diesel generators"); PLC kontrol paneli (Wikimedia Commons: "Category:Programmable logic controllers") | 3:15–4:30]
> Let's answer the first question. Why didn't the generators start?
>
> Google's preliminary report — the company's best understanding at the time, and explicitly subject to change — says both the primary and the backup automation systems failed to start the generators. So backup power never reached the load.
>
> Read that again. There was a backup. And there was a backup for the backup. Both of them failed to do the one thing they exist for.
>
> Engineers have a name for this: a common-cause failure — when supposedly independent safety systems fail together because they share something. A design, a piece of software, a setting, a sensor. Google hasn't yet published exactly what that shared weakness was. Its final report is still pending, and I'm not going to guess.
>
> But it isn't a freak event. The Uptime Institute, which studies data-centre failures, has repeatedly found on-site power problems to be among the most common causes of serious outages. Not hackers. Not software. Electricity.
>
> Which brings us back to the mystery of the three zones.

### [SAHNE 7 | Animasyon: Üç zon; biri kararıyor, trafik okları diğer ikisine yığılıyor, onlar da kırmızıya dönüyor | 4:30–5:40]
> When one zone dies, the theory is simple: traffic moves to the other two. In a mobile network, we see the same thing. When a mast goes down, the neighbouring cells absorb its phones. If those neighbours were already busy, they start to struggle too.
>
> Google's preliminary account, as reported, describes something similar: as workloads failed over, they overloaded other zones in the region. Every system tried to restart, reconnect and catch up — all at once.
>
> Then there's geography. Many British firms keep their data in the UK for legal, contractual or customer reasons. Google has one cloud region in the UK. So if the London region is struggling, there's no second British region to escape to. Some firms were resilient against losing a zone — but not against a bad day for the whole region.
>
> So, what does this mean for whether it happens again?

### [SAHNE 8 | Gerçek görsel: Bank of England binası (Wikimedia Commons: "Bank of England building" – telifsiz lisanslı mimari fotoğraf) → animasyon: onlarca banka logosu yerine nötr ikonlar, hepsi tek bir buluta bağlı | 5:40–6:55]
> Here's the answer to our third question. Banks, courts and couriers didn't all choose the same building on purpose. They each chose a cloud provider — and there are only a handful of big ones. Separate decisions, the same hidden dependency.
>
> British regulators have been worried about exactly this. Since January 2025, the UK has had a regime for so-called critical third parties: it lets the Treasury designate firms like cloud providers so that the Bank of England, the Prudential Regulation Authority and the Financial Conduct Authority can oversee their resilience directly.
>
> After this outage, the FCA said it was engaging with Google and affected firms. Reuters reported that European financial regulators also questioned Google. Google apologised and said it would investigate.
>
> The uncomfortable truth is that it's not enough for each bank to be resilient. The system they all lean on has to be resilient too.

### [SAHNE 9 | NOC ekran duvarı tekrar; sonra cüzdan içinde iki farklı kart ve biraz nakit (Pexels stok) | 6:55–8:05]
> So let's close the loop. How does a building designed for power cuts lose to a power cut? Not because nobody planned for it. Because the plan depended on one shared step — the automation that starts the generators — and both versions of that step failed together.
>
> In my operations centre, we have a rule. We don't test whether the backup exists. We test whether it starts — under load, on a bad day, with the real switch, not the theory.
>
> And for you, the lesson is smaller but practical. Keep a second way to pay: a card from a different bank, and a little cash. When one hidden system fails, you want your Tuesday morning to depend on two of them, not one.
>
> Google's final report will tell us what that shared weakness really was. When it lands, we'll come back to it. Until then — the cloud is a building. And buildings need their generators to start.
