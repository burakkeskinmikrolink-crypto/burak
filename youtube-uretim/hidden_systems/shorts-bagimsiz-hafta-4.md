**VO:**
> One lightning strike. One million homes.
> 9 August 2019. Lightning hit a transmission line north of London. That alone is routine; the grid is built for it. But within moments, an offshore wind farm and a gas power station both lost output, each linked to the strike. Frequency fell so far that automatic protection disconnected more than a million customers to protect everyone else. Some trains stopped. Both generators later paid 4.5 million pounds each into a redress fund. A routine fault, a rare combination:

The site's fire suppression system was out of service. One warning, left for years, and…

> If Britain's entire grid went dark, you couldn't just switch it back on. Most power stations need electricity to start. So the system operator keeps a plan, once called black start, now called restoration. A few special plants that can start on their own come up first. They power a small piece of the network, connect some homes, and build a stable island. Then islands join up, one by one. Engineers rehearse it, hoping never to do it. Because someone has to know: how do you restart…

**VO:**
> The alarm failed silently.
> August 2003, Ohio. In a utility control room, the software that should have warned operators about problems stopped working, and nobody realised. Power lines sagged into trees and tripped, one after another, while the screens looked calm. Within hours, a cascade cut power to about 50 million people across the northeastern United States and Ontario. The US–Canada investigation listed the silent alarm among the causes, alongside tree trimming and training. In a control room, the worst sound is no sound. Because sometimes…
