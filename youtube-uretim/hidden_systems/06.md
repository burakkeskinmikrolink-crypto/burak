## Neden bu konu (not)
7–8 Mayıs 2026'da Kuzey Virginia'daki bir AWS veri salonunda soğutma çöktü. Sunucular kendilerini korumak için kapandı ve Coinbase saatlerce işlem yapamadı. Görünmeyen sistem burada soğutma: buluttaki her watt elektrik ısıya dönüşüyor. 04. bölümle (Google Londra, elektrik) aynı "bulut aslında bir bina" çizgisinde ama farklı bir arıza modu anlatıyor. Ağustos'ta Nebius'ta da fırtına yüzünden soğutma kesildi, yani konu tekil bir olay değil.

> ⚠️ **Yayın öncesi kontrol:** AWS'nin resmî post-event summary'si bu oturumda bulunamadı. Başlangıç saati (16:20 PDT mi, 17:25 PDT mi) ve toplam süre (12 saat ile 28 saat arası) kaynaklar arasında farklı. Metin bu yüzden "early evening", "well into the next day" gibi ifadeler kullanıyor. "Multiple zones" ifadesi **Coinbase'in** açıklaması. AWS tek zon diyor ve metinde iki taraf ayrı ayrı veriliyor.

## Senaryo

### [SAHNE 1 | Siyah ekran → termometre ibresi yükseliyor (Pixabay: "thermometer rising"); kırmızı kripto grafik ekranı donuyor (kendi grafik, gerçek borsa arayüzü yok) | 0:00–0:15]
> A room in Virginia got too hot — and one of America's biggest crypto exchanges stopped trading for most of a night. By the end of this video, you'll know why heat, not hackers, is the cloud's oldest enemy.

### [SAHNE 2 | NOC ekran duvarında sıcaklık grafikleri (Pexels: "server monitoring dashboard") | 0:15–0:21]
> I work in a mobile network operations centre, where we watch temperature as closely as traffic.

### [SAHNE 3 | RECONSTRUCTION: Uzun sunucu koridoru, soğutma ünitelerinin fanları yavaşlıyor; ardından telefonunda uygulamaya bakan bir kullanıcı, yüz yok (Wikimedia Commons: "Category:Data centers"; Pexels stok) | 0:21–0:55]
> It's early evening in Northern Virginia, on Thursday, the seventh of May 2026. Inside one of Amazon Web Services' data halls, the air-conditioning — industrial cooling, the size of rooms — starts to fail. Not one unit. According to Coinbase's later account, several cooling units failed at the same time.
>
> Thousands of servers keep working. And every one of them keeps doing the only thing a computer does with electricity: turning it into heat.
>
> The temperature climbs. And then the machines do something sensible. They protect themselves. They switch off.
>
> A few hundred miles away, someone opens the Coinbase app to make a trade. It won't go through.
>
> So here's the question at the heart of this story: how does a building full of the world's most advanced computers get beaten by… warm air?

### [SAHNE 4 | Animasyon: Elektrik → sunucu → ısı → soğutma döngüsü; "18–27°C / 64–81°F" bandı (kendi grafik) | 0:55–2:05]
> Here's the physics nobody puts in the adverts. Almost all the electricity a server consumes ends up as heat. A data centre is, in a very real sense, a giant electric heater that happens to do maths.
>
> So every data hall is built around a second, hidden system: cooling. Fans and air handlers push cold air through the racks and pull hot air out. Chillers or cooling plant carry that heat outside. The industry body ASHRAE recommends keeping the air going into servers roughly between eighteen and twenty-seven degrees Celsius.
>
> If cooling stops, there is no grace period measured in hours. The heat keeps arriving at full speed. So servers carry thermal protection: once temperatures cross a safe limit, they shut down rather than cook their own components.
>
> To a customer, that looks exactly like a power cut. AWS described it as a thermal event that caused a loss of power to some of its EC2 servers and EBS storage volumes in a single availability zone, known as use1-az4, in its US-East-1 region.
>
> But that's where the story splits. AWS said one zone was affected. So why did Coinbase say it saw failures across more than one?

### [SAHNE 5 | RECONSTRUCTION: Gece vardiyası, tek bir mühendis silüeti ekranlar önünde; dışarıda soğutma kuleleri (Wikimedia Commons: "Category:Cooling towers") | 2:05–3:15]
> Before we answer that, let's stay in the building — because the recovery is its own lesson.
>
> When a power failure ends, you can often switch things back on. When a cooling failure ends, you can't. The room is still hot. Bring the servers back too soon and they'll trip again, or be damaged. Engineers have to restore cooling, wait for temperatures to fall below the thresholds, and only then bring hardware back, carefully, in stages.
>
> One published timeline says cooling was only stabilised back to normal levels the following afternoon. Meanwhile, AWS said it shifted traffic away from the affected zone for most services, and suggested customers with urgent needs restore from backups or launch in other zones. Load balancers and other networking services were reported degraded.
>
> Coinbase moved in stages too: first "cancel-only" mode, then an auction mode, then full trading. In its own retrospective, it put the disruption to core trading at around eight hours. FanDuel and CME Group were also reported among the companies affected.
>
> And here's our next question. Why does so much of the internet keep living in this one corner of Virginia?

### [SAHNE 6 | Animasyon: Üç kutu "Zone A / B / C". İki zonlu bir sistemde biri kararıyor, "oy çokluğu" kayboluyor; üç zonlu sistemde iki zon çoğunluğu koruyor (kendi grafik) | 3:15–4:30]
> First, let's answer the zone mystery — as far as the public record allows.
>
> Coinbase said its systems were designed to survive the loss of a single zone, but that it "observed failures impacting multiple AWS zones." AWS's public description centred on one zone. Both can be partly true: a problem can start in one building and still ripple through the services that depend on it.
>
> What Coinbase did next is revealing. It said it would move a key messaging system — Kafka, which carries the stream of events through its platform — from two zones to three.
>
> Why three? Here's the general principle, used across the industry. Many systems keep data consistent by voting: a change only counts when a majority of copies agree. With two locations, lose one and there's no majority left. With three, you can lose one and the other two still outvote the silence.
>
> In my world, that's the difference between a backup and a quorum. A backup waits for you to switch to it. A quorum keeps deciding, even when part of it is gone.

### [SAHNE 7 | Gerçek görsel: Loudoun County, Virginia veri merkezi binaları (Wikimedia Commons: "Data centers in Loudoun County") | 4:30–5:40]
> Now — why Virginia?
>
> US-East-1, in Northern Virginia, is Amazon's oldest cloud region, dating back to 2006. Over two decades, an enormous amount of the internet has been built there, and the region has become one of the densest clusters of data centres in the world.
>
> That concentration has a history. In December 2021, the same zone ID, use1-az4, lost power, according to reporting at the time. In October 2025, a separate fault in US-East-1 — involving the DNS system for its DynamoDB database service — triggered one of the most widespread outages in AWS's history.
>
> Three different causes. One region. When so much depends on one place, even routine failures make headlines.
>
> And there's a reason to think heat, specifically, is becoming a bigger problem. So: is the cloud getting hotter?

### [SAHNE 8 | Gerçek görsel: sıvı soğutmalı sunucu rafı ve borular (Wikimedia Commons: "Category:Liquid cooling"); fırtına bulutları (NASA/NOAA kamu malı uydu görüntüsü) | 5:40–6:50]
> In a sense, yes. The chips used for artificial intelligence draw far more power than ordinary servers, and industry surveys, including the Uptime Institute's, report that rack power densities are rising. More power in a rack means more heat to remove from the same few square metres. That's pushing data centres toward liquid cooling, which is more efficient but adds yet another system that has to keep running.
>
> And cooling depends on the outside world. In August 2026, the cloud provider Nebius published a post-mortem describing how a storm knocked out cooling at a data centre in its US-Central-1 region, and how a separate software bug made the recovery slower.
>
> So cooling isn't a background utility. It's a front-line dependency — as critical as power, and arguably less understood.

### [SAHNE 9 | NOC ekranı; tek bir sıcaklık grafiği yükseliyor, sonra sakinleşiyor | 6:50–7:55]
> So, let's close the loop. How does warm air beat the world's most advanced computers? Because every watt becomes heat, and when cooling fails, the only safe move for the servers is to switch themselves off.
>
> Why couldn't they come straight back? Because you have to cool a room before you can wake it.
>
> Why did a "single-zone" problem hurt more than one zone? Coinbase blamed failures it saw across zones, and rebuilt a key system around three zones instead of two — so a majority survives.
>
> And why Virginia? Because the internet grew up there.
>
> In my operations centre, a temperature alarm is never a minor alarm. It's a countdown. The cloud may feel weightless, but it has a thermostat — and on a warm evening in May, the thermostat won.
