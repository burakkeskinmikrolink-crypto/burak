## Short 10 — 25 Kas | Tek gizem: Takvimin bozduğu bulut (~35 sn)

| Zaman | Görsel | Ekran yazısı |
|---|---|---|
| 0–2 sn | **Hareketli:** Takvim yaprağı "29 FEB" sayfasında duruyor, altında "2013" yazınca kırmızı X | A CALENDAR DATE BROKE AZURE. |
| 2–12 sn | Sertifika simgesi: "valid until: +1 year" | CERTIFICATES VALID FOR ONE YEAR |
| 12–24 sn | "2012 + 1 = 29 Feb 2013" → "DATE NOT FOUND" | A DATE THAT DOESN'T EXIST |
| 24–35 sn | Sunucu simgeleri "unhealthy" olarak sararıyor | SERVERS MARKED UNHEALTHY |

**VO:**
> A calendar date broke Azure.
> 29 February 2012. Microsoft's cloud created security certificates that were valid for one year. To work out the end date, the code simply added one to the year. On leap day, that produced 29 February 2013, a date that doesn't exist. Certificate creation failed, servers were marked unhealthy, and the problem spread across parts of Azure. Microsoft published a detailed account afterwards. The fix was simple. Finding it at scale wasn't. Every four years, engineers remember the day…

**Döngü:** "…the day…" → "A calendar date broke Azure."

**VO:**
> The grid runs on spinning metal.
> Big power-station generators are heavy, and they spin in step with the grid. If one power station suddenly trips, all that spinning mass keeps turning for a few seconds, slowing how fast the frequency falls. NESO calls this inertia, and it buys the control room time. Wind and solar connect through electronics, so they don't provide it naturally. That's why Britain now runs machines that generate nothing at all. They just spin, because…
