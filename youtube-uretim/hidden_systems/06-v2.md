> A room in Virginia got too hot — and a major crypto exchange stopped trading for hours. Here's why heat, not hackers, is the cloud's oldest enemy.

### [SAHNE 2 | Kontrol odası, ekranlarda sıcaklık grafikleri (Pexels) | 0:12–0:17]
> This channel is made by someone who works inside a mobile network operations center.

### [SAHNE 3 | Ekran yazısı: "Northern Virginia · 7 May 2026". RECONSTRUCTION: sunucu koridoru, soğutma ünitesi fanları yavaşlıyor (Wikimedia Commons: "Category:Data centers"). Köşe göstergesi: 22°C → 27°C | Kesme: 3–4 sn | 0:17–0:45]
> Early evening in an Amazon Web Services data hall. The industrial cooling starts to fail — according to Coinbase's later account, several cooling units at once.
>
> Thousands of servers keep turning electricity into heat. The temperature climbs.
>
> So how does a building full of advanced computers get beaten by… warm air?
>
> Then the machines do something sensible. They switch themselves off.

### [SAHNE 4 | Animasyon: Elektrik → sunucu → ısı → soğutma döngüsü; "18–27°C / 64–81°F" bandı (kendi grafik). Köşe göstergesi: 32°C | 0:45–1:55]
> Here's the physics nobody puts in the adverts. Almost all the electricity a server consumes ends up as heat. A data centre is, in a very real sense, a giant electric heater that happens to do maths.
>
> So every data hall is built around a second, hidden system: cooling. Fans and air handlers push cold air through the racks and pull hot air out. Chillers or cooling plant carry that heat outside. The industry body ASHRAE recommends keeping the air going into servers roughly between eighteen and twenty-seven degrees Celsius.
>
> If cooling stops, there's no grace period measured in hours. The heat keeps arriving at full speed. So servers carry thermal protection: once temperatures cross a safe limit, they shut down rather than cook their own components.
>
> To a customer, that looks exactly like a power cut. AWS described a thermal event that caused a loss of power to some EC2 servers and EBS storage volumes in a single availability zone, known as use1-az4, in its US-East-1 region.
>
> But the story splits here. AWS said one zone was affected. So why did Coinbase say it saw failures across more than one?

### [SAHNE 5 | RECONSTRUCTION: Gece vardiyası, ekranlar önünde siluet; dışarıda soğutma kuleleri (Wikimedia Commons: "Category:Cooling towers"). Köşe göstergesi yavaşça düşüyor: 32°C → 24°C | 1:55–3:05]
> Before answering that, stay in the building, because the recovery is its own lesson.
>
> When a power failure ends, you can often switch things back on. When a cooling failure ends, you can't. The room is still hot. Bring the servers back too soon and they'll trip again, or be damaged. Engineers have to restore cooling, wait for temperatures to fall below the thresholds, and only then bring hardware back, carefully, in stages.
>
> One published timeline says cooling was only stabilised back to normal levels the following afternoon. Meanwhile, AWS said it shifted traffic away from the affected zone for most services, and suggested customers with urgent needs restore from backups or launch in other zones.
>
> Coinbase moved in stages too: first "cancel-only" mode, then an auction mode, then full trading. In its own retrospective, it put the disruption to core trading at around eight hours. FanDuel and CME Group were also reported among the companies affected.
>
> For customers, those hours are what an outage really costs. Not the moment of failure, but the long, careful wait while a building cools down.
>
> And that raises another question. Why does so much of the internet keep living in this one corner of Virginia?

### [SAHNE 6 | Animasyon: İki zonlu sistemde biri kararıyor ve "MAJORITY?" soru işareti beliriyor; üç zonlu sistemde iki zon "✓" ile çoğunluğu koruyor | 3:05–4:20]
> First, the zone mystery, as far as the public record allows.
>
> Coinbase said its systems were designed to survive the loss of a single zone, but that it "observed failures impacting multiple AWS zones." AWS's public description centred on one zone. Both can be partly true: a problem can start in one building and still ripple through the services that depend on it.
>
> What Coinbase did next is revealing. It said it would move a key messaging system, Kafka, which carries the stream of events through its platform, from two zones to three.
>
> Why three? Here's the general principle, used across the industry. Many systems keep data consistent by voting: a change only counts when a majority of copies agree. With two locations, lose one and there's no majority left. With three, you can lose one and the other two still outvote the silence.
>
> Network engineers describe this as the difference between a backup and a quorum. A backup waits for someone to switch to it. A quorum keeps deciding, even when part of it is gone.

### [SAHNE 7 | Loudoun County, Virginia veri merkezi binaları (Wikimedia Commons) → harita: Kuzey Virginia'da yoğun noktalar (Natural Earth tabanı) | 4:20–5:30]
> Now, why Virginia?
>
> US-East-1, in Northern Virginia, is Amazon's oldest cloud region, dating back to 2006. Over two decades, an enormous amount of the internet has been built there, and the region has become one of the densest clusters of data centres in the world.
>
> That concentration has a history. In December 2021, the same zone ID, use1-az4, lost power, according to reporting at the time. In October 2025, a separate fault in US-East-1, involving the DNS system for its DynamoDB database service, triggered one of the most widespread outages in AWS's history.
>
> Three different causes. One region. When so much depends on one place, even routine failures make headlines.
>
> And heat, specifically, may be becoming a bigger problem. Is the cloud getting hotter?

### [SAHNE 8 | Sıvı soğutmalı raf ve borular (Wikimedia Commons: "Category:Liquid cooling"); fırtına bulutları (NASA/NOAA kamu malı uydu görüntüsü) | 5:30–6:40]
> In a sense, yes. The chips used for artificial intelligence draw far more power than ordinary servers, and industry surveys, including the Uptime Institute's, report that rack power densities are rising. More power in a rack means more heat to remove from the same few square metres. That's pushing data centres toward liquid cooling, which is more efficient but adds yet another system that has to keep running.
>
> Cooling also depends on the outside world. In August 2026, the cloud provider Nebius published a post-mortem describing how a storm knocked out cooling at a data centre in its US-Central-1 region, and how a separate software bug slowed the recovery.
>
> There's also the weather itself. Cooling plant has to push heat out into the air outside, so the hotter the day, the harder it has to work — and the less spare capacity it has left when something else goes wrong. A hot evening is exactly when a cooling failure hurts most.
>
> So cooling isn't a background utility. It's a front-line dependency — as critical as power, and arguably less understood.

### [SAHNE 9 | Köşe göstergesi büyüyüp tüm ekranı kaplıyor, sonra normal değere iniyor; kontrol odası | 6:40–7:45]
> In network operations, a temperature alarm is never treated as minor. It's a countdown. Every watt is still arriving as heat, and the only safe move for a server is to switch itself off.
>
> That's what happened in Virginia: cooling failed, servers protected themselves, and a room had to be cooled before it could be woken. Coinbase, which said it saw failures across zones, rebuilt a key system around three zones instead of two, so a majority survives. And all of it happened in a corner of Virginia where the internet grew up and never quite left.
>
> The cloud feels weightless. It isn't. It has fans, pipes, chillers and limits. It has a thermostat.
>
> And on a warm evening in May, the thermostat won.
>
