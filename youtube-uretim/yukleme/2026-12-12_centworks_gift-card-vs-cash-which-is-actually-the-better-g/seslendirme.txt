To answer that, we'll score them across five rounds: value, loss, rules, risk, and feelings. [pause 900]

Round one: value to the person who gets it.

In 1993, the economist Joel Waldfogel asked students to estimate what their holiday gifts had cost, and how much they personally valued them. On average, they valued gifts at around eighty-four percent of the price. He called the missing part the deadweight loss of Christmas. Picture a fifty-dollar sweater you'd only have paid forty for yourself. That ten-dollar gap is the loss.

His conclusion was that cash is the most efficient gift, because the person can buy exactly what they want.

But that study has been argued about ever since. Other researchers found that many people value gifts at or above their price, and that the giver's thought counts for something. Economists are still divided.

A gift card sits in the middle. It's money, but only at one store, or one kind of store. If that's a store your niece loves, a gift card is nearly as good as cash. If it isn't, it isn't.

Round one goes to cash, but only narrowly. But what happens to all that money nobody spends? [pause 1000]

Round two: money that gets lost.

Every year, Bankrate surveys Americans about unused gift cards. In its 2024 survey, forty-three percent of adults said they had at least one unused gift card, voucher or store credit. The average amount was about two hundred and forty-four dollars per person. Added up, that's roughly twenty-seven billion dollars.

Some of it is small leftover balances, like six dollars and twelve cents, that never quite match a purchase. Some is forgotten in a drawer. Some is for a store nobody goes to anymore.

When that money goes unspent, it doesn't vanish. The store or the card issuer keeps it. The industry has a name for it: breakage.

Some people sell unwanted gift cards on resale sites, but usually for less than the balance. That's another quiet loss.

Cash doesn't have breakage. A twenty at the bottom of a drawer is still a twenty.

Round three: the rules.

Since 2010, a federal law called the Credit CARD Act has protected most gift cards sold in the U.S. Money on a covered card generally can't expire for at least five years from the date it was issued, or the last time money was loaded onto it.

Fees are limited too. A store can't charge inactivity or maintenance fees until the card has gone unused for at least twelve months. After that, it can charge no more than one such fee a month, and the fees must be disclosed on the card or packaging.

Some states go further, with stronger protections.

There's a catch with the cards that look most like cash: bank-branded gift cards that work almost anywhere. They're flexible, but many charge a purchase fee, often several dollars, when you buy them. That fee comes out of your gift budget.

So the rules are better than most people think. Round three is closer to a tie. But is a gift card riskier than cash? [pause 1000]

Round four: risk.

Cash has one big risk: lose it, and it's gone. No one can freeze an envelope.

Gift cards have different risks. The newest is card draining. Thieves open cards on store racks, copy the numbers, and reseal them. When someone loads money onto the card, they spend it first. There's a step-by-step breakdown of how it works linked in the description.

And if a store chain goes out of business, its gift cards can become hard or impossible to use.

You can cut most of the gift card risk. Buy from behind the counter, or directly from the retailer's own website. Check the packaging. And keep the receipt, because it's your proof if something goes wrong. Some cards can also be registered online, which can help if the card itself gets lost.

Round four goes to cash, with a caveat: for a teenager, cash can also disappear in other ways. So why do so many people still choose gift cards? [pause 1000]

Round five: feelings.

According to the National Retail Federation, gift cards were the second most popular holiday gift in 2025, with spending expected to reach about twenty-nine billion dollars. People keep choosing them, even though round one says cash is more efficient.

Why? [pause 1000] Because a gift card says, "I know what you like," and cash can feel like you didn't have time to think. A gift card also nudges someone to spend on a treat, not on bills.

Researchers who defend gift-giving point out that the giver gets something too: the pleasure of choosing, and the message it sends.

For some families, cash is perfectly normal. For others, it feels awkward. That part isn't economics. It's relationships.
