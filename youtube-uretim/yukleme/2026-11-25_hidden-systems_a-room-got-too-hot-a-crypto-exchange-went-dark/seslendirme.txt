Then the machines do something sensible. They switch themselves off. [pause 900]

Here's the physics nobody puts in the adverts. Almost all the electricity a server consumes ends up as heat. A data centre is, in a very real sense, a giant electric heater that happens to do maths.

So every data hall is built around a second, hidden system: cooling. Fans and air handlers push cold air through the racks and pull hot air out. Chillers or cooling plant carry that heat outside. The industry body ASHRAE recommends keeping the air going into servers roughly between eighteen and twenty-seven degrees Celsius.

If cooling stops, there's no grace period measured in hours. The heat keeps arriving at full speed. So servers carry thermal protection: once temperatures cross a safe limit, they shut down rather than cook their own components.

To a customer, that looks exactly like a power cut. A W S described a thermal event that caused a loss of power to some EC2 servers and EBS storage volumes in a single availability zone, known as U S E one, A Z four, in its US-East-1 region.

But the story splits here. A W S said one zone was affected. So why did Coinbase say it saw failures across more than one? [pause 1000]

Before answering that, stay in the building, because the recovery is its own lesson.

When a power failure ends, you can often switch things back on. When a cooling failure ends, you can't. The room is still hot. Bring the servers back too soon and they'll trip again, or be damaged. Engineers have to restore cooling, wait for temperatures to fall below the thresholds, and only then bring hardware back, carefully, in stages.

One published timeline says cooling was only stabilised back to normal levels the following afternoon. Meanwhile, A W S said it shifted traffic away from the affected zone for most services, and suggested customers with urgent needs restore from backups or launch in other zones.

Coinbase moved in stages too: first "cancel-only" mode, then an auction mode, then full trading. In its own retrospective, it put the disruption to core trading at around eight hours. FanDuel and CME Group were also reported among the companies affected.

For customers, those hours are what an outage really costs. Not the moment of failure, but the long, careful wait while a building cools down.

And that raises another question. Why does so much of the internet keep living in this one corner of Virginia? [pause 1000]

First, the zone mystery, as far as the public record allows.

Coinbase said its systems were designed to survive the loss of a single zone, but that it "observed failures impacting multiple A W S zones." A W S's public description centred on one zone. Both can be partly true: a problem can start in one building and still ripple through the services that depend on it.

What Coinbase did next is revealing. It said it would move a key messaging system, Kafka, which carries the stream of events through its platform, from two zones to three.

Why three? [pause 1000] Here's the general principle, used across the industry. Many systems keep data consistent by voting: a change only counts when a majority of copies agree. With two locations, lose one and there's no majority left. With three, you can lose one and the other two still outvote the silence.

Network engineers describe this as the difference between a backup and a quorum. A backup waits for someone to switch to it. A quorum keeps deciding, even when part of it is gone. [pause 900]

Now, why Virginia? [pause 1000]

US-East-1, in Northern Virginia, is Amazon's oldest cloud region, dating back to 2006. Over two decades, an enormous amount of the internet has been built there, and the region has become one of the densest clusters of data centres in the world.

That concentration has a history. In December 2021, the same zone ID, U S E one, A Z four, lost power, according to reporting at the time. In October 2025, a separate fault in US-East-1, involving the DNS system for its DynamoDB database service, triggered one of the most widespread outages in A W S's history.

Three different causes. One region. When so much depends on one place, even routine failures make headlines.

And heat, specifically, may be becoming a bigger problem. Is the cloud getting hotter? [pause 1000]

In a sense, yes. The chips used for artificial intelligence draw far more power than ordinary servers, and industry surveys, including the Uptime Institute's, report that rack power densities are rising. More power in a rack means more heat to remove from the same few square metres. That's pushing data centres toward liquid cooling, which is more efficient but adds yet another system that has to keep running.

Cooling also depends on the outside world. In August 2026, the cloud provider Nebius published a post-mortem describing how a storm knocked out cooling at a data centre in its US-Central-1 region, and how a separate software bug slowed the recovery.

There's also the weather itself. Cooling plant has to push heat out into the air outside, so the hotter the day, the harder it has to work — and the less spare capacity it has left when something else goes wrong. A hot evening is exactly when a cooling failure hurts most.

So cooling isn't a background utility. It's a front-line dependency — as critical as power, and arguably less understood. [pause 900]

In network operations, a temperature alarm is never treated as minor. It's a countdown. Every watt is still arriving as heat, and the only safe move for a server is to switch itself off.

That's what happened in Virginia: cooling failed, servers protected themselves, and a room had to be cooled before it could be woken. Coinbase, which said it saw failures across zones, rebuilt a key system around three zones instead of two, so a majority survives. And all of it happened in a corner of Virginia where the internet grew up and never quite left.

The cloud feels weightless. It isn't. It has fans, pipes, chillers and limits. It has a thermostat.
